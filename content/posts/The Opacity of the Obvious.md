## Obviousness Arrives and Erases Its Own History

When I first studied economics, its most basic ideas were, for months, actively counterintuitive. I could follow the definitions, but to work with them I had to hold them in place by force. Then the feeling turned over. The ideas didn't just come to seem correct; they came to seem obvious, and I lost the ability to recover what had ever been hard about them. The obviousness arrived and erased its own history. I can still make the case for it: ask me why this way of modeling an equilibrium is the compelling one and the reasons come readily. But I had those reasons in hand long before they convinced me; I could derive the result while it still felt wrong. So what I've started to doubt is whether the reasons I now offer are what convinced me, or something I assemble now to answer the question. The unsettling part isn't that I can't produce reasons. It's that the reasons I produce may be partly built after the fact, and the conviction may never have come from them at all.

There are two things we call obvious. Some obviousness is public and checkable: the truck is bigger than the car; anyone can look. But the kind that matters here is stranger. It feels just as settled, and there's nothing public to point to: anyone can check my derivation, but not whatever turned it from correct into obvious.

I would like to think of myself as someone who can give an account of what I believe: point to the evidence, retrace the reasoning that brought me there. For most of my opinions I more or less can. But the economics isn't the exception. A great deal of what I'm surest of arrives the same way, as the plain shape of the world rather than as anything I concluded, and when I stop and ask how I actually know, there's nothing under the certainty I can reach. The emptiness doesn't dent the clarity. When I examine some beliefs I expect to find a series of 'therefores', but I often find just an 'of course'. What kind of learning can leave a result so settled that the path that made it has disappeared?

That's a question for the biology of learning, and part of the answer had already been in front of us. The first essay showed one thing that learning leaves behind: a single neuron that comes to answer to Jennifer Aniston's face, her name, and her voice at once, a concept left by encounters the cell no longer holds. What I hadn't looked at was how an experience gets turned into that kind of structure. The brain turns out to split the work. One system catches the episode; the one we end up reasoning with is changed by it and keeps no copy.

## The Slow System Keeps the Shape and Loses the Episode

A rat runs a familiar track. As it moves, cells in its hippocampus fire in sequence, each tuned to a particular place, so the firing traces the route the way a finger follows a line on a map. That night, while the rat sleeps, the same cells fire in the same order, compressed into milliseconds, over and over. Matthew Wilson and Bruce McNaughton reported this in 1994: the brain was replaying the day's route in its sleep.[^replay]

The hippocampus is the brain's fast system for episodes, good at catching what happened this time, in this place, in this order. The cortex learns more slowly, and it cares less about preserving the episode than about extracting what repeats across cases. Replay is one way the fast system tutors the slow one. The hippocampus keeps feeding episodes back into circulation, and each pass changes the cortex a little.

What ends up in the cortex is a change in the landscape through which later input will move. It keeps none of the event in its original form. Replay is like a lecture, and what the cortex keeps is what a student still has years later: not the lecture word for word, only the shape it left behind. The analogy stops there, though, because with a lecture there's at least a transcript somewhere. Here there's no transcript that the shape was abstracted from. There's only the altered terrain.

That terrain is the one the earlier essays have been mapping. A concept is a valley in it — a low place the system rolls into and settles in, carved by everything that recurred across many encounters. When a face you've never seen registers instantly as a face, that's an input landing on its slopes and rolling to the floor.

It's natural to picture the hippocampus keeping a copy of the day until the night's replay turns it into shape. It doesn't keep copies either. Part of it is wired back onto itself into a small landscape of valleys, the same kind of landscape as the first essay's.[^ca3] And the work of turning a fresh experience into a lasting change in the brain, which neuroscientists call consolidation, starts almost at once. Within hours the experience has stopped being activity and become a change in the strength of connections, and a connection strength isn't a place a scene can sit. So bringing back this morning is already falling into a valley, just as it will be in ten years, and nothing in how vivid the memory feels tells you how far it has drifted.[^talarico]

Both systems hold shape, then. What differs is what cut it. A valley cut by one occasion catches that occasion. A valley cut by a thousand catches the commonality in the occasions and no single one of them. The loss comes from the second kind, because a valley is made of nothing but the history that dug it and contains no record of that history.

James McClelland, Bruce McNaughton, and Randall O'Reilly worked out what the division of labor buys you: hippocampus and cortex are tuned for jobs that can't be done by one system at once.[^cls] The hippocampus gives each episode a fairly separate neural signature, so that today's lunch doesn't smear into yesterday's (neuroscientists call this pattern separation). That's the right design for recording what happened and the wrong one for noticing what usually happens. The cortex runs the other way. Similar inputs reuse many of the same cells, which is what generalizing requires: the next chair is recognized through cells that earlier chairs already shaped. Their name for the pairing is complementary learning systems: the two are complementary because neither could do the other's job.

Luria's mnemonist, Solomon Shereshevsky, shows what it would cost in a life to have only the first kind of memory.[^luria-mnemonist] He could hold pages of words and numbers and return them years later almost intact. But the same undimmed particulars made abstraction hard. Metaphor tripped him; the gist of a passage stayed buried under everything he couldn't help retaining. In one reported sequence, 1, 2, 3, 4, then 2, 3, 4, 5, then 3, 4, 5, 6, and so on, he failed to notice the simple shifting structure. Instead, he saw each number as a vivid particular, a character or image, and spent as long memorizing the logical sequence as he did memorizing random strings of digits. A mind that keeps every detail equally bright struggles to let cases blur into a kind, and that blurring is what a concept is. Some differences have to be allowed to stop mattering.

Running both systems, a brain can remember the case without losing the kind, and recognize the kind without crushing the case. The Jennifer Aniston neuron from the first essay showed what this second kind of learning leaves. It was a changed way of responding, not a stored photograph: her face, her name, her voice, and her name in print came to answer to one another. The learning was what many encounters left behind, not the keeping of any one.

Building a concept and consolidating a memory run on one rule. In the cortex, similar inputs run through overlapping cells, so what recurs across encounters gets reinforced and what varies washes out. That is the rule that carved the chair valley. It's also the rule the cortex is running when replay feeds it episodes. Sleep makes it nearly visible: episodes are reactivated in overlapping combinations, and the overlap is what gets strengthened.[^gist] Consolidation is the making of a kind out of cases, which is why what the cortex ends up holding is a gist and not a copy.[^semanticization]

The loss follows from that. Many episodes go in; one structure comes out. A process that sends many things to one thing can't be run backwards — nothing in the result records which of the many produced it. That's a logical necessity, and it's why the argument here doesn't stand or fall with the two-system picture. Any way of earning a general pattern from particular cases pays the same price. The biology adds the news that this is how much of our thinking gets built.

Call this the severance: **episodes can shape later thought without remaining available as grounds.** A system can be formed by its encounters and keep nothing from which they could be rebuilt. The case has done its work by becoming part of the pattern.

## The Cost of Abstraction

The detail does go missing. An old memory feels worn down, generic, less *there* than it was, and the natural way to describe that is erosion, a recording fading on its tape. That description catches the feeling and misses what is actually happening. What looks from a distance like a recording wearing down is really experience being remade into something that no longer contains it.

We do still have episodic memories: I can remember a particular party, a particular sentence, a particular face at a particular table. But the cortical pattern that lets me read parties as parties isn't itself a retrievable party. The cortex didn't file the party from five years ago and then let the file rot. What it drew from many parties does work no stored episode could. It lets you walk into a room you've never entered and read within seconds how the evening will go. It lets you see a face you've never seen and know at once that it's a face. It lets you finish a sentence someone else began. None of that comes from a particular evening; it comes from what a great many evenings trained you to expect.

This is the tradeoff the two systems exist to manage, and it's not a defect waiting for better engineering. Memory for the single case and the ability to see the pattern pull in opposite directions. To find a pattern at all, a system has to stop treating each episode as singular. It has to treat non-identical things as the same for some purpose, and treating them as the same means letting some differences drop out. The concept is born out of that refusal to keep every difference. Think again about the chairs from an earlier essay — five, fifty, five thousand, no two of them alike: somewhere in there a learner settles on which differences not to care about, and once that settles, it stops feeling like a choice. It feels like the way chairs are.

The phrase *for some purpose* is the key. A different purpose would have kept different differences and sorted the same encounters into a different kind. Nothing in the episodes themselves announces which sorting is the right one; the purpose comes from outside them, from what the learner needed the category to do.

It's tempting to call this compression — even lossy compression, like a low-quality MP3 made from an original recording — as though the cortex had saved the episodes in a smaller format and could unzip them later. But compression keeps the original around in shrunken form, and the originals aren't around. Learning a concept leaves a changed readiness for the next case, and that's why it doesn't run in reverse. You can't recover the footsteps from the slope they wore into a hillside. The pattern left behind can still track the world well enough to guide action, prediction and correction. What disappears is the ability to retrace how it formed.

This is the part that unsettled me most. The cortex, where most of the thinking happens, runs on the changed readiness and not on the episodes that changed it. Now turn that on yourself. Take a view you hold firmly — say that a person's worth doesn't depend on what they produce. Someone asks why you believe it, and without deciding to, you reach down for the grounds, sure there's something there to reach. You assume you could lay out the evidence, walk the steps from it to the conclusion, keep what you believe apart from why.

Are the reasons you'd give now the ones that first moved you toward the view? Probably not, and nothing much rides on that. Could you go back and recover the originals, the encounters that did the work — the moment, the person, the failure that first set the conviction? You want the answer to be yes; you feel yourself wanting it. And then the question with teeth: while you hold the view, right now, do you have any access to what is convincing you? The first is idle: the reasons you'd give now could differ from the originals and still be good ones. The second is about memory, whether that history can be recovered. The third bites because it's about now, not then. It isn't asking whether the belief can be defended, only whether anything you can reach is what's doing the convincing.

A quiet assumption runs underneath the whole exchange: that the grounds stay in view, on call to the mind that holds them, so that a conviction can always be traced back to its source. Whatever is rational in a belief is supposed to be recoverable as reasons. Listen to yourself the next time someone asks why. You answer as if reporting the grounds, and the grammar of the exchange takes for granted that the belief and its basis are still joined.

This assumption is common ground between camps that agree on almost nothing else. The empiricist thinks the grounds bottom out in the evidence of the senses; the rationalist thinks they bottom out in truths the mind can just see to be so. They quarrel over what counts as a ground, but both assume the knower can, in the end, present those grounds — and that the descent ends on a floor you could stand on, raw sense-evidence or self-evident content, something finally given. But if the path from episode to concept runs one way, then for most of what we know there's no return path to walk.

Nothing slipped away through carelessness. Some episodes are still on hand, held briefly in the fast system or written down before they faded, so we can walk back a step or two and sometimes further. But that recoverable layer is shallow, and beneath it the thinking runs on structure that kept no record of how it formed. One layer down we can still give grounds. Several layers down we aren't withholding the grounds; we no longer have them. Thinking that has become fluent runs without effort or felt deliberation, the way a skilled reader moves through a sentence without sounding it out. Nothing in the experience of fluent thinking tells us which layer we're on. It feels exactly the same whether the grounds are one layer down or ten.

This is why the economics I fought to learn could come, later, to feel obvious. The months of strain were the cortex being reshaped, and once reshaped it kept the competence and not the record of acquiring it. The struggle left a readiness that no longer remembers being hard.

Those layers come from the way learning stacks. Each round of learning happens inside a mind that earlier learning has already shaped, so each round builds on the last — readiness built on readiness, each layer laid down by episodes the next layer never saw.[^schema-speed] And the turning of experience into structure runs on more than one timescale. Over hours, the brain chemically locks in the synaptic changes an experience caused — synaptic consolidation. That step preserves rather than generalizes: it makes a change durable enough for the slower work to act on. The slower work runs over months and years, as the memory is progressively rebuilt in cortex, leaning less on the fast system and more on the slow one — systems consolidation. That's the step where cases become kinds. And during childhood the same cortical learning runs on tissue still unusually plastic, laying structure down faster and deeper than it ever will again.[^synaptic][^squire-remote] Two mechanisms and one unusually plastic stretch of life, all running in one direction: experience turned into readiness, and no route back. The mind gains the kind by losing the case.

If memory were the whole of it, the damage would be contained. It gets worse in two directions. Look earlier, at perception itself, and what I take to be raw intake turns out already shaped by something learned, before any thinking starts. Look wider, at how much of what I reason with I never acquired at all, and a great deal of it proves to be inherited, compressed by other minds or by the species and handed over already formed.

## Perception Was Never Raw

The two squares are the same shade of gray. Edward Adelson's checkerboard sets a board on a surface and stands a cylinder beside it so that its shadow falls across part of the board. One square, call it A, sits in open light; another, B, sits inside the shadow. A photometer reports that the two send the same amount of light to your eye. Your eye refuses the report. B looks plainly lighter. The visual system has worked out that B is a pale square lying under a shadow, which is almost certainly the truth about the board, and it hands you that conclusion as the way the squares simply look.[^adelson]

![Adelson's checker-shadow illusion: squares A and B are the same shade of gray, but B, lying in the cylinder's shadow, looks plainly lighter.](../Adelson.png)

The strange part is how the illusion survives knowing. Read the explanation, cover the rest of the image with your hand, check the two patches against an instrument, and the moment you uncover them they part again into light and dark. The regularity that a shadowed surface throws back less light has been built into the visual system by evolution and by early experience of surfaces and shadows, so the system applies what it's already learned, below the level where you could decline. The correction isn't something vision performs and then submits to you for review. The inference is the experience.

The suspicion that perception is no clean window is old, and one of the skeptics' stock cases runs the opposite way from Adelson's board. A straight oar looks bent where it enters the water, and it goes on looking bent there even after you've pulled it out, measured it straight, and put it back. Here the visual system corrects nothing: light bending at a water surface isn't a regularity it was built around, so it hands you the raw appearance and leaves the fixing to your intellect. That's the old skeptical case, where the senses slip and reason catches them. Adelson's board is harder to escape because the shadow correction is one the system *was* built around. It has already done the interpreting before anything reaches you to check. The perception is doing its job exactly, and the interpretation is folded into the success.

The McGurk effect makes the same point across two senses at once. Film a person mouthing the syllable *ga* while you play the sound *ba* through the speakers, and most people hear *da*. The brain has settled a disagreement between the eyes and the ears by choosing the syllable that best explains both, and it does this without telling you. Close your eyes and you hear *ba*; open them and the sound itself changes to *da*, with no sense of anything happening in between.[^mcgurk] The brain does the same thing in time. Light reaches your eyes almost at once; sound trails behind it, more so the farther off its source. Watch someone split wood a short way off and the thunk still lands on the falling axe, not a beat after it; move far enough away and the sound finally peels loose and arrives late, the way thunder lags lightning. While the lag stays short enough, the brain folds the two senses into one event and never shows you the gap it closed. You don't feel the lag and then correct for it. The synchrony is delivered, the same way the syllable was.

How short is short enough isn't fixed. Waka Fujisaki and colleagues exposed people to a steady audiovisual lag for a few minutes, the sound trailing the light by the same gap each time, and afterward their sense of simultaneity had slid toward that lag: pairs they would earlier have judged out of step now read as falling together.[^recalibration] What counts as one moment is a setting, retuned by whatever the senses have lately received. The present I live in is built, and rebuilt, and it arrives looking like the plain fact of now.

Attention adds a different problem. The question is no longer only how input is interpreted but which input is let into experience at all. Daniel Simons and Christopher Chabris filmed two teams passing basketballs and asked viewers to count the passes made by the team in white. If you haven't seen it, try it before reading on.

About half never saw the person in a gorilla suit walk into the middle of the scene, stop, beat its chest, and walk off, nine seconds in all. Shown the film again with no task, they see the gorilla at once and can hardly believe they missed it.[^simons-chabris] What's missing here is registration, not brightness or volume. They didn't see the gorilla faintly. As far as anyone can tell, they didn't see it at all.

The three cases work differently, but they point one way. Experience arrives already worked over — by expectations about how light behaves, by learned bonds between sight and sound, or by attention bent to a task. One important family of models generalizes the point, under the name predictive processing. In the work of Rajesh Rao and Dana Ballard in the late 1990s, and Karl Friston's later development of it, perception is treated as inference — not deliberate reasoning, but the brain estimating what in the world would best account for the signals reaching the senses.[^rao-ballard-friston] It brings expectations to the input, weighs them against what arrives, and updates when the gap grows large enough. What reaches experience is the system's best account of what produced the signal. The details are contested; the shape is enough. The world we perceive is already worked over by the structures through which we receive it. They do the shaping, and they finish before anything reaches me to check. The same gap opens here as in memory: the interpreting is already done, and it leaves no record I can consult.

This makes one old suspicion easy to inhabit: that there may be no clean layer of experience lying beneath interpretation, no raw given for the rest to be built on.[^not-yet-thesis] Philosophy calls that layer the Given. Wilfrid Sellars turned the doubt about it into an argument, and we'll come back to him.

The perception cases leave something out: shaped perceptions don't all feel alike. Some I can talk myself out of with a little effort. Others sit so deep that trying to doubt them brings on something closer to vertigo than to doubt. The difference is depth, how much it would take to move us, and it runs along a continuum. Shallow structures stay available as beliefs you could state and argue with. Deeper ones sink into perception and fluency, and feel less like beliefs than like reality itself. The deepest stop feeling like conclusions at all and become the conditions under which anything looks like anything. Which raises a more basic question: where did I get these structures? The deeper one sits, the less of it was ever mine to learn.

## Much of What I Think I Never Learned

Even after memory and perception, something seems safe. Maybe the first contact with the world isn't raw, maybe memory keeps less than I imagine, but the framework I think with is still mine. It was built from what I've seen, read, heard, practiced, and learned, and I can retrace enough of it to justify what I believe. Even if I can't recover every step, there's still some continuous line from my experience to my present thought.

But the line severs.

Not only because I forget. Forgetting would leave the picture mostly intact: the evidence was mine, the reasoning was mine, and only the record has gone missing. The deeper problem is that much of the structure I now think from was never available to me as evidence in the first place. The structure I think with runs from what I learned myself, to what was handed to me already formed, to what was never mine to learn. From inside, none of it announces its source. It arrives with the same impact: this is just how things are.

The friendliest case is expertise, because there at least I really did acquire the structure myself. Chess expertise has been measured since William Chase and Herbert Simon's work in the early 1970s. Show a master a position from a real game for five seconds and she will rebuild it almost perfectly. Show her the same pieces scattered at random and she does little better than a beginner. She is seeing configurations, the pawn chains and piece relations that recur across thousands of games, and she sees them the way you see a face. Enough training turns into perception.[^chase-simon][^gobet] The master doesn't infer the threat. She sees it.

This is structure built by experience but not kept as experience, the same division the complementary systems draw. The master's eye was made by thousands of positions, corrections, mistakes, and games, and those episodes were consolidated into cortical structure, into a changed readiness for the next board. When she looks now, the episodes don't reappear as grounds. The shaping stayed; the episodes didn't. They've become a way of seeing. The line from experience to judgment is there, but only as cause — the same severance, this time worn into a single expert eye.

Social norms do the same thing, more quietly. A norm can settle into the body until it no longer feels like a rule at all. What counts as rude, childish, professional, intimate, vulgar, cruel, masculine, feminine, serious, unserious: these are absorbed through correction, imitation, embarrassment, approval, exclusion, repetition. They're rarely learned as stated rules and then accepted after inspection. A person doesn't usually think, my culture has trained me to experience this gesture as disrespectful. The disrespect is simply there.

Language works on us in a similar way. In the 1930s Benjamin Lee Whorf, studying Hopi and working with Edward Sapir at Yale, argued that languages don't merely express thought, they shape it.[^whorf] The strong version, that your language fixes what you can think, is now largely set aside. Speakers of languages without a future tense have no trouble thinking about tomorrow. Speakers of languages without a word for *purple* still see the color.[^pinker-critique]

A weaker version survived, and the evidence for it has built up, unevenly, over thirty years. Languages don't determine thought. They bias it. They train attention onto the features their grammar makes speakers track, and the features the grammar lets you ignore go unmarked and become harder to notice.[^absolute-spatial][^relativity-evidence] The effects are real but graded.

A clear example comes from Tuyuca, a language of the Colombian and Brazilian Amazon. In Tuyuca a verb has to mark how the speaker knows what they're reporting: whether they saw it, heard or sensed it some other way, inferred it from evidence, were told it, or are assuming it. *He played soccer* isn't a complete utterance. You have to choose between *I saw him play soccer*, *I have evidence he played soccer*, *someone told me he played soccer*, and so on. English can mark those distinctions, but it doesn't require them. Tuyuca does. A child learning the language is doing more than attaching words to thoughts already formed. The grammar makes the speaker mark, every time, the separation between content and source, until a distinction English leaves optional becomes part of ordinary fluency.[^evidentiality] A Tuyuca speaker reports, with every claim, how she came by it. What the grammar records is the last step — I saw it, I was told — and the habit of recording it was absorbed the way a norm or a chess eye is, without a record of its own. Even a language built to keep track of grounds reaches one layer down.

Disciplines do the same thing more explicitly. To learn economics, law, medicine, physics, or literary criticism is to acquire more than information. It's to acquire a trained sense of what counts as a problem, what counts as an explanation, what can be ignored, what has to be controlled for, what sort of answer would feel elegant or evasive or crude. After enough training, the discipline is no longer something one applies from the outside. It becomes part of the seeing. That's why an outsider can hear an expert explanation and understand every sentence while missing what made the explanation compelling. None of this means the classifications are arbitrary. A trained physician may see a real danger a layperson misses. A legal category may separate cases that should be treated differently. And formation is the most powerful thing we do, not a flaw to apologize for. Nearly everything we count as expertise or progress is formation that worked, a hard-won way of seeing that lets a trained mind reach what an untrained one can't. The danger isn't that formation exists. The danger is forgetting that formation and reconstruction are different things.

Keep going and the line to my experience breaks even more sharply, because some of the deepest structures aren't learned within a single life at all.

At the far end sit things taken on so early that no one recalls taking them on: that a solid thing won't pass through another solid thing, that one is not two. An infant's grip on object permanence and a master's eye for a position aren't the same accomplishment, yet from the inside they wear the same face. Each arrives as immediate. Each shapes perception before explicit reasoning starts. Each, when its holder is asked to justify it, can only be pointed at: I just see it that way. The structure doing the work has gone invisible, which is what makes the seeing feel direct.

Renée Baillargeon's drawbridge studies make the point sharply. Show a four-month-old a flat screen rotating upward, with a block sitting in its path that the rising screen hides from view, and the baby expects the screen to stop where the block is. Rig it to seem to pass straight through and the baby stares longer, as though a rule had been broken. Elizabeth Spelke and her collaborators spent decades cataloguing such early expectations: that objects persist, that one object is not two, that a moving thing can set a struck thing in motion, that agents are tracked by their goals rather than only by their paths.[^baillargeon][^spelke] They show up so early and so uniformly that many researchers take them to be starting points rather than lessons.

The neuroscience backs this up. The cortex that houses these starting points isn't a blank surface waiting for experience to write on it. Hubel and Wiesel found visual cortex already organized into regular columns in kittens that had not yet seen, ahead of the experience that later refines it.[^inherited] Michael Arcaro and Margaret Livingstone sharpen the point. The cortex comes with a biased scaffold: a rough map, laid out like the image on the retina, that fixes roughly where regions for particular categories, such as faces, will later sit. But those regions don't fully form without the matching experience. Macaques raised without ever seeing faces develop the place where face areas would normally emerge, but not the face areas themselves. The brain isn't born knowing faces. It's born with a rough plan for where face-shaped input will be taken up, if it arrives.[^inherited]

Janet Werker showed that six-month-olds can hear phoneme contrasts from many languages, and that by twelve months they have mostly lost the contrasts their own language doesn't use.[^werker] The narrowing isn't simple forgetting. It's a setting being fixed while the input is still arriving, and once it's fixed, distinctions the child's language ignored become genuinely hard to hear. The standard case is the English *r* and *l*. Patricia Kuhl's group tested infants in Seattle and in Tokyo: at six to eight months the two groups told *r* from *l* about equally well, and by ten to twelve months the Seattle infants had got better at it while the Tokyo infants had not.[^kuhl-rl] The adult who grew up speaking only Japanese, where the contrast does no work, often can't hear the gap between *right* and *light* at all, and has to be trained back toward a difference they could once register without effort. The infant began wider than the adult will end. Experience did not write the contrasts in from nothing; it selected, from a range already standing, which ones to keep and sharpen.

The inherited layer and consolidation are different things. Consolidation is what a developed cortex does to episodes it has lived through; inherited structure is what the cortex brings before there's anything personal to consolidate. From the inside, though, they come to the same thing. Each gathers the world's variety into stable responses. Each makes some moves easy and others unavailable. Each offers no record I can consult. A valley doesn't announce what dug it. It simply feels like the plain shape of things.

What varies along this range is how easily a structure can be revised. A novice can pick up a new chess pattern in a week. A social norm can turn over in a generation, so that what once felt indecent or unprofessional comes to feel arbitrary or cruel. A disciplinary framework can change across decades. Other structures barely move. That objects persist, that a cause comes before its effect, that one thing is not two: these can be poked at in thought experiments, but they aren't unseated by ordinary learning.

Special relativity is my favorite example of something I accept that the rest of my mind refuses. The mathematics is plain enough that anyone willing to spend a few hours can follow it: we can't say whether two distant events happen at the same time without saying who's asking. It depends on how you're moving. Picture a train rushing past a platform, and two bolts of lightning that strike its front and its back. Someone standing on the platform, exactly halfway between the two strike points, sees both flashes arrive at the same instant and says the strikes happened at once. Someone seated at the middle of the train is moving toward the front flash and away from the back one, so the front light reaches her first, and she says the front strike came earlier. Neither of them is mistaken. There's no fact about which strike came first that sits above the two frames and settles it. For events far enough apart and close enough in time, even the order can flip with how you are moving. I can reproduce the math on demand, but it has never become intuitive, never become obvious the way the economics did, never arrived as perception. The perceptual line between together and apart can be retuned in minutes, as Fujisaki's subjects showed; what doesn't move is the expectation that there's always a fact about whether two events happened at once. That expectation is built into a great deal else — into counting, into causation, into what it is for something to happen at all — and no amount of evidence I've met has dislodged it in me.

That's what a deep structure feels like when the intellect has accepted one thing and the rest of the mind goes on expecting another. The new claim can be believed, used, taught, calculated with. But it hasn't become terrain. It hasn't altered the background against which the rest of thought moves.

This is the severance again. It isn't a clean break between thought and world, and it isn't a claim that belief is arbitrary. The problem isn't absence of cause: the world, other people, evolution, and my own experience all helped shape the structure. The problem is the difference between cause and ground: a structure can be caused by a history without making that history available as evidence. This is easy to miss, because the system is so good at its work. The cortex synthesizes so much, and so well, that we can usually reconstruct enough of a justification to feel the belief has been grounded, and often that's good enough to act on. We've even built tools that do it better — records, proofs, footnotes — holding grounds still outside the mind where formation can't reach them. None of it closes the gap. The severance is real, and it sits underneath the very fluency that hides it.

That's why the feeling of obviousness is so misleading. It presents itself as immediate, as though thought had reached something simple, a beginning. It's the opposite: what remains after experience, inheritance, and culture have done their work and vanished into the readiness they left.

## But Couldn't You Just Remember Why?

One hope is still standing. The point of running two systems was that the slow one need not carry everything, because the fast one, the hippocampus, was built to catch episodes whole. So perhaps the cortex lost my grounds and episodic memory kept them. When I need the basis of a belief, maybe I can turn back to the episodes themselves and read off what put it there. If I can, the severance is real but survivable, because the system that loses the case is backed by the one that keeps it.

It is a reasonable hope, and it fails twice over.

It fails first because the episodes aren't waiting to be consulted. The hippocampus isn't an archive the cortex draws on at leisure; it catches an episode and spends it tutoring the cortex, replaying it until the lesson has been transferred, and what the cortex builds it keeps no copy of. Over months and years a memory comes to lean less on the hippocampus and more on the cortex, and the episode that did the teaching isn't retained alongside the competence it produced. For the great bulk of what I know there's no episode left to return to, only the readiness it trained. The reservoir the rescue counted on is mostly empty.

It fails again where an episode does seem to return, because recall isn't retrieval. Memory rebuilds. Frederic Bartlett showed this in the 1930s by having people read an unfamiliar folk tale, "The War of the Ghosts," and retell it after a delay.[^bartlett] What came back was a reconstruction: the strange parts smoothed into something the teller's own world could hold, the gaps filled with what should have been there, the whole run through schemas that were themselves the residue of earlier learning. Much later the mechanism turned out to be almost literal. A reactivated memory becomes briefly unstable and has to be written back down, so the act of remembering can quietly rewrite the thing remembered.[^reconsolidation] What returns is built now, on equipment shaped by everything learned since.

So even the part of the reply that works doesn't deliver what was wanted. Asked why I hold a view, I can almost always produce an answer: premises, a line of support, a conclusion. It can be clean, it can be sound, and the grounds it cites can genuinely bear on the belief. The temptation is to call that recovery, as if I had walked back down the path that first convinced me and read off its steps. I haven't. I can reconstruct a justification; I can't recover the history that formed the belief. But if the reconstruction is logically sound — if the premises really do support the conclusion — does it matter how I got there? The two can come apart completely: a reconstruction can be sincere, logically sound, and still not be the process that produced the conviction. A better logician builds a better ladder, and a better ladder is still not the ascent. Nothing in the fluency tells me which one I'm holding, which is why they're so easy to confuse.

Whether it matters depends on what's being asked. For whether the belief is justified, maybe not: if the premises really carry the conclusion, the ladder may be all the support it needs. For me it does. I can accept the gap on paper and still find that it grates, because the reasoner I take myself to be, the one who can give an account of what he believes, is supposed to be moved by the reasons he gives. The ladder may hold. It just isn't how I climbed.

## Philosophers of the Missing Ground

The disturbance from the opening — a conviction that arrives already obvious and offers no record of how it formed — looked at first like a curiosity about me. I suspect it is a small case of an old philosophical problem: thought can't fully trace where it came from.

One question runs under all of this: what may a mind claim about grounds it can no longer reach? Five people walked into it from different directions.[^thin-slices] They came at the problem with different equipment and different aims, and hit the same wall.

The trouble is that some of the deepest grounds of what I think are not available to me as grounds at all. They have been turned into what I think with. Looking backward, this shows up as loss: where did this structure come from, and why is the trace gone? Looking forward, the same fact shows up as obviousness: the structure has become transparent, and I see through it to a world that simply seems that way. Loss and obviousness are two faces of one process, the settling of history into readiness. This isn't a comfortable thing to notice, and the thinkers I keep returning to are the ones who noticed some version of it and tried to say what follows.

The oldest is as old as philosophy. The skeptics began with perception, where contact with the world feels most direct, and pointed out that the same tower looks round and square, the same water warm and cool, the same color different by the hour. Their conclusion was modest by later standards and still unsettling: what presents itself as reality may already be an interpretation. That's the outward-facing form of the severance, the doubt about whether the senses hand over the world. It would take a long time for anyone to turn the same doubt inward and ask whether the mind hands over its own grounds.

Wilfrid Sellars gave the inward turn its sharpest modern statement. His question wasn't whether experience matches the world but whether it can hand the mind a ground at all. In a 1956 essay he attacked what he called the Myth of the Given: the idea that knowledge rests on a layer of pure, pre-conceptual experience — a patch of red simply had, before any judgment is made.[^sellars]

The notion is incoherent, he argued, because a patch of red is already a classification. To register it as red at all is to set it against other colors, to be able to find it again — to have entered what he called the space of reasons, the network where something counts as evidence only by standing in relations to other things you know. There's no innocent floor beneath that network.

The perception cases make his target easy to see: it isn't only that we sometimes misread the data; there may be no raw data of the kind the old picture wanted, no floor of pure experience. Sellars was no biologist, and his argument isn't the predictive-processing story. What the science adds is a reason in the tissue why the raw layer isn't there to be recovered.

Kant had already seen that the structuring goes deep. Experience arrives already organized by structure that experience itself couldn't have supplied: things come to us in time and space, as causes and effects, as countable. Before I can test any particular claim against experience, experience has to show up for me as an ordered world in the first place. Kant placed that ordering in the knower, called it transcendental, and treated it as the fixed conditions of any possible experience — a floor that makes findings possible without being a finding itself.

The science backs him about the depth, not about the floor. It keeps his sense that thought has deep conditions, but asks whether some of those conditions might be formed, inherited, tuned, and retuned rather than fixed once and for all. If the structures that make experience possible are themselves partly the product of biology, learning, and history, then the old line between what experience gives us and what we bring to it starts to blur. Some structures may feel transcendental only because they're old, deep, and built into the cognition we happen to have. Depth needn't be a hard floor. It can be a slope that has become too steep to climb down from inside.

Ask me to defend something I'm merely fairly sure of and I can give you reasons. Ask me to defend the claim that an assertion and its flat denial can't both be true at once, the thing I'd stake everything on, and I have almost nothing to say. Any argument I could offer would already have to assume it. The conviction I hold most absolutely is the one I'm least able to ground, and the inversion is a clue to how belief is arranged. My guess is that this is what the oldest and deepest valleys feel like from inside: so much has been built through them that nothing is left to stand on to argue for them.

Quine, I believe, saw the shape of it. He kept the depth Kant had found and gave up Kant's floor: in "Two Dogmas of Empiricism," in 1951, he argued that no statement faces experience alone, that the whole web of belief meets the world together.[^quine-dogmas] At the edges sit claims easily revised by observation. At the center sit the ones held almost immovable, logic and arithmetic and the principles everything else assumes, held there because too much depends on them, not because they belong to a special category of truth. When experience pushes back, we choose what to give up by how much else would have to move with it. That's the shape of our valleys. What Quine's picture lacked was an account of how the shape got there, and learning that piles up over a lifetime supplies one. The center is central because later thought was built through it, so that to abandon it is to disturb everything constructed on the assumption that it would hold, not merely to drop a sentence.

Quine's web gives the ladder question from earlier a stronger form. Perhaps justification was never about where a belief came from, only about how it hangs together with everything else we believe now. Then the lost origins don't matter, and the severance is harmless. That holds — for a step. Then ask what is doing the hanging-together. The web coheres by habits of response, by what feels important, by which categories carve the cases, by what counts as relevant. Those are doing the judging, and they're exactly the consolidated, inherited equipment the earlier arguments showed I can't get behind. The floor I was reaching for hasn't disappeared; it has moved down one level and is waiting there. The point is not that defending a belief requires access to its origins. It's that even present justification, coherence and all, runs on structure I can't reconstruct.

An old enough text can read as not merely wrong but, in places, unintelligible, the reasoning refusing to land because its author is making moves inside a shape you can't stand in. A long conversation with someone formed in a very different culture can end the same way, in an orientation you can describe and not occupy.

These feel like curiosities of reading or of travel. They're the severance showing up between minds rather than inside one. Different formations can change what counts as an object, a question, an explanation, or a relevant piece of evidence in the first place — which goes deeper than ordinary disagreement about what to believe.

Michel Foucault opens *The Order of Things* with his laugh at a list, taken from Borges, of a supposed Chinese encyclopedia that sorts animals into those belonging to the Emperor, the embalmed, the tame, sirens, those drawn with a fine camel-hair brush, and those that from a long way off look like flies.[^foucault] The laugh is the recognition of a way of carving the world he can read but can't inhabit. The joke runs back onto me as I write this, because I can't read Foucault in the original either: *The Order of Things* comes to me roughly the way that list came to him, distilled through others.

His worked case, from an earlier book, *The Birth of the Clinic*, is medicine. Before about 1800, European medicine mostly sorted: a disease was a type you located in a table of symptoms, matched by its outward signs. Then medicine started looking inside the body. Doctors like Xavier Bichat traced disease to damage in particular tissues, and the patient became a case in which a hidden anatomical truth could be uncovered. What changed was not only what doctors knew but what could count as medical knowing at all.

Historical categories work this way. They don't merely change what people believe; they change what can show up as a candidate for being true or false. It's easy to overstate — Foucault is often read as saying truth is a fiction, and that's not the claim worth keeping. A category can be historically made and still track the world. A modern diagnosis of pneumonia really is better than a Renaissance diagnosis of an excess of phlegm, not merely different from it. What's true is that the better diagnosis became reachable only inside a trained way of attending, classifying, and running a clinic. What feels medically obvious is, in large part, trained historical seeing.

Then there's science, which is supposed to be the exception.

In the summer of 1947 Thomas Kuhn, then a graduate student in physics, was preparing a case study on the history of mechanics for a science course aimed at non-scientists. He sat down to read Aristotle's *Physics* and found it not merely wrong but unreadable: distinctions that seemed to track nothing, explanations that explained nothing. Which was strange, because the same author was plainly brilliant everywhere else.[^kuhn-aristotle] He puzzled over it and got nowhere.

Then, one afternoon at his desk, it gave way. The distinctions started tracking. The explanations started fitting. He could see that Aristotle hadn't been writing bad early physics at all; he'd been writing coherent physics of another kind, in a landscape that took different things as basic. Kuhn hadn't learned a new fact. The terrain he was reading from had changed shape.

My economics, Foucault's laugh, and Kuhn at his desk are three versions of one event: a trained obviousness that won't travel directly from one formed mind to another, or from an earlier self to a later one. Kuhn's version matters most, because it closes the obvious escape route. You can accept everything above and still say: fine, ordinary people are formed, but science just follows the facts. Science does have facts. It also trains the eye for which fact would matter, which measurement would count, which pattern would be worth explaining at all.

What came out of that afternoon was Kuhn's account of how scientists are made.[^kuhn-structure] One piece of it he called the exemplar: the worked problem a student solves again and again until it stops being a problem and becomes a way of seeing. Students don't become physicists, chemists or biologists by reading laws and deriving applications from first principles. They work problems — inclined planes, pulleys, projectiles, buffer solutions, titration curves, equilibrium constants, genetic crosses, model organisms. Each one is a shaped encounter, and enough of them train the eye. The student starts to see *this case* as *that kind* of problem, calling for these moves, these simplifications, these measurements, these ignored details.

The sense that a step is obvious is the residue of that drilling. "Therefore" is what a visible path feels like from inside. "Of course" is what a valley feels like from inside. Even science trains the eye it then trusts.

These thinkers aren't saying the same thing. The skeptic doubts the senses; Sellars denies the innocent Given; Kant finds fixed conditions where Quine finds only a gradient; Foucault makes the categories historical and Kuhn trains the scientist. But each arrives at the same place: press an "of course" for its "therefore," and it is unclear what answers.

## The Given Was the Result

The valleys aren't a film over thinking that we might learn to wipe away. They are cognition. The opacity here is one reason intelligence works at all. A creature that had to verify the origin of each category before using one wouldn't get through breakfast.

The strange thing isn't that the system sometimes fails. The strange thing is how well it works. It's behind perception, speech, expertise, law, science, art, moral life, and nearly everything we call human progress. A doctor sees a danger in a body because training has changed what can stand out. A lawyer hears a distinction because the law has trained one distinction rather than another into relevance. A musician hears where the phrase should go. A mathematician feels that the next step is available before she has written it out. None of this is a departure from reason. It's much of what reason looks like in a creature like us.

But a system can work well without being transparent to itself. Inside one mind, the severance is rarely a practical problem; almost nothing depends on knowing where a valley came from, and the system carries us anyway. It surfaces only when thought turns back on itself and asks for the grounds of the structures it has been using all along. The better the cortex does its work, the less of its own history it leaves behind to be inspected. A structure can carry me reliably through the world and still leave me unable to say what formed it.

The old hope looked beneath formation for an untouched Given that would end the asking and bear the weight, and there's nothing under there to find. The Given was never the ground we stand on before we begin. It is the shape the ground took while we were not looking.

The same severance has an outward face. When my "of course" meets someone else's contrary "of course," there's no shared floor we can climb down to and settle the matter on, because the reasons each of us would give already run on the structure in dispute: a way of carving the world one can describe and can't occupy, not an error waiting to be corrected. That's where the severance costs most. What settles such a dispute, when anything does, isn't a floor either of us reaches but the world pushing back over time; that's how pneumonia came to beat phlegm. Even then, what crosses from one mind to the other is the conclusion and not the shape that made it obvious, and how little of a settled mind survives that crossing is a question for a later essay.

The failures are where we get to see any of this: the illusion that survives explanation, the old text that won't make sense, the physics we can calculate but never feel. In each, a structure that's normally invisible shows as structure.

This essay sits inside what it describes. Its shape looks like the obvious one to me because my own training makes it look that way, and someone formed elsewhere, working through the same findings, would build it differently and see what I've stepped over. That doesn't refute it. It's an instance of it. The footnotes are the essay's own attempt to hold a little of the trace outside a mind that can't keep it, and they leave the rest of my formation exactly where the argument says it sits, behind me, doing its work unseen.

Let's go back to the economics. Ask me why I believe one of those results and I can hand you everything: the assumptions, the equations, every step that carries the premises to the conclusion. It's a clean account and it holds. But I had all of it long before it convinced me. I could run the derivation while the result still felt wrong, and then, months later, the wrongness turned over into obviousness without a line of the math changing. So the equations can't be what moved me; I was holding them, unmoved, the whole time. What changed was me, the terrain the same steps now run through. Yet when someone asks why I believe it, I give them the derivation. But they asked what convinced me, and I've handed them a proof I already had back when nothing was convinced. I'm not answering them. I'm really not answering them.
