## Turning the Lens on Morality

Watch someone catch sight of real cruelty — a child struck, a helpless animal tormented. The recoil comes first, whole and certain, before a single reason has been assembled. Moral judgment feels sharp from the inside. And yet it moves with context, history, attention, and training. Feeling certain and yet shifting by degrees: the last two essays found exactly that combination in concepts and in belief.

Those essays built a way of seeing: concepts and beliefs as attractor valleys carved by learning, with smooth, continuous change underneath judgments that feel all-or-nothing. Once that picture was on the table, I started thinking about ethics.

Turn the same lens on morality. The question isn't which actions are right or wrong; the wiring alone can't answer that. It's what the wiring underneath moral judgment helps us see, the way the wiring underneath belief did in essay 2.

---

## Feeling Gets There First

Hume made a claim in 1739 that moral philosophy has argued about ever since: reason alone can't move you. Sentiment moves you. By *sentiment*, I mean the felt pull of approval or disapproval, the emotional force that makes an action strike you as admirable, disgusting, cruel, noble, or wrong. When you witness cruelty and feel that it's wrong, you aren't working through an argument. You're having something closer to a perceptual experience. The wrongness hits you before the reasoning starts.

Reason can tell you what's the case. It can tell you that an action will cause suffering, or that a particular lie will be discovered. It can't tell you to care. To get from "this action causes suffering" to "I should not do this," you need something other than reason, and Hume's name for that something is sentiment. Reason, in his famous phrase, is the slave of the passions.

Jonathan Haidt has a useful demonstration of this. He calls it moral dumbfounding.[^haidt-2001] He gives subjects a vignette about Julie and Mark, an adult brother and sister who, on vacation together, decide to have sex one time. They use two forms of contraception. They enjoy it. They agree never to do it again, and they never tell anyone. Subjects who read the vignette report strong moral disapproval. When asked why, they offer reasons. They could have a deformed child; but they used contraception. Someone might find out; but no one does. It might damage their relationship; but in the vignette it doesn't. Each reason runs into the way the case was built. The judgment doesn't move with the reasons. Often, people reach moral conclusions first and find reasons second; when the reasons fail, they find new reasons rather than changing the conclusion.[^c3-dumbfounding]

If that's roughly right, the work happens before reasoning catches up, and reasoning isn't doing it. Whatever produces the sentiment is doing what matters.

Hume saw this too, and tried to describe what produces the sentiment. He proposed that we catch one another's feelings the way you catch the mood of a room — a theory of emotional contagion that anticipates modern work on mirror neurons and on empathy as shared feeling — and that this raw catching needs a corrective, because on its own it reaches the people near you and stops. He also noticed that some moral responses seem to grow straight out of feeling while others only take hold with social scaffolding behind them.[^hume-terms] These were the right kind of proposal: descriptions of how moral judgment works, not arguments about how logic says it should work. But they pointed at something going on in the brain that Hume had no way to see.

As a description, it has always struck me as right that the judgment arrives as a feeling and the justification comes after. Our language is a useful trace of the psychology. We call actions repulsive, filthy, sickening, chilling, cold-blooded. We talk about dirty money, tainted evidence, crushing guilt, and freezing someone out. Moral disapproval borrows the language of disgust, temperature, pressure, illness, and recoil. That doesn't settle the theory. It does suggest that moral reactions live close to the senses and to feeling.

So I'll take Hume as a working assumption, in the form the evidence supports: moral judgment starts as feeling, and reasoning arrives afterwards. That's a claim about where moral judgments come from, not about whether anything in the world answers to them.

Notice that this is far from a moral theory. Suppose the whole neural story turns out right — the learning, the basins, all of it. You now know why you feel that cruelty is wrong. Does that tell you whether it *is* wrong? Here it's tempting to think the explanation has done more than it has. If we can trace a moral conviction back to learning in the brain and to evolutionary pressure, haven't we shown it's *just* a feeling, with no claim on the truth?

We should resist that. Where a belief came from doesn't settle whether it's true. A map can be drawn by a fallible cartographer and still get the coastline right. So knowing that a basin was carved by learning and selection tells you the *cause* of a moral conviction, not its *content* or its *truth*.

Philosophers have a live argument, and a good one, about whether knowing where a moral judgment came from undermines its claim to knowledge or leaves it untouched.[^c3-realists][^c3-street] The description here doesn't need it settled. It isn't an attempt to derive a moral philosophy from the biology, and it's useful whichever way that argument comes out. If our moral feelings track something real, this is how they track it. If they're the crooked timber of humanity, something reason has to work against, then this is the grain it's working against.

At this point I should say something about how I see morality. . I don't think moral judgments answer to facts that would hold whether or not anyone felt anything about them: I reject moral realism. The description that follows doesn't depend on that. A realist can read it as an account of how our feelings come to track the moral facts; I read it as an account of where the feelings come from and what they do. Where my view does make a difference is in what I can say when someone asks whether a whole culture's morality was wrong, and I come back to that below.

## Feeling Learns

Two processes have run through concepts and belief so far: neurons learning by firing together, and options competing until one settles. Both show up again in the circuitry of feeling, and they run the same way there.

### Moral Reactions Run Through Feeling, Fast

Empathy, in the sense of feeling what someone else feels, runs through the anterior insula and parts of the cingulate, regions involved in bodily feeling, pain, conflict, and whatever registers as emotionally urgent.[^anterior-insula] In a 2004 study by Tania Singer, sixteen romantic couples came in for scanning. The woman lay in the scanner; her partner sat alongside, with both of their hands visible to her through a mirror. On some trials a small electric shock was delivered to the back of her hand. On other trials a cue indicated her partner's hand was being shocked instead. In both conditions her anterior insula and anterior cingulate lit up, and the activations overlapped.[^empathy-pain] Watching a loved one in pain ran through some of the same circuitry she used to feel pain herself.

Moral reactions run through the circuitry of feeling too, and they run fast. Jean Decety and Stephanie Cacioppo recorded high-density EEG while people watched short clips of someone getting hurt, sometimes on purpose and sometimes by accident. Both kinds of clip show an injury, so a difference in how the brain responds to them is a response to something other than the sight of harm: most plausibly the intent behind it, the part that makes it a moral matter. A model used to infer where inside the head the signal came from placed that difference near the amygdala at around 122 milliseconds, and in ventromedial prefrontal cortex at around 182; when the harm was accidental, neither region responded this way.[^moral-eeg] Moral evaluation runs through the circuitry of feeling in under a fifth of a second — too fast for deliberate reasoning to have done the work.[^eeg-caveat]

None of this yet shows feeling working the way belief does. What it shows is where moral reaction happens: in the circuitry of feeling, and early, within the first fifth of a second. The question is whether that circuitry learns and settles the way the rest of the brain does.


### Do We Learn to Feel?

In Joseph LeDoux's work on fear conditioning, rats heard a tone paired repeatedly with a mild shock. After enough pairings, the tone alone produced freezing. Recordings from individual neurons in the lateral amygdala, part of the brain's threat-detection circuitry, showed that specific cells had come to respond more strongly to the tone once it predicted the shock.[^fear-conditioning] The standard reading is that the connections carrying the tone had been strengthened because they were active at the same time as the signal from the shock.[^la-hebbian] That's Hebbian plasticity, in different circuitry. So emotional associations get written in by the same basic rule that writes conceptual associations into cortex.

### Value and Perception Settle the Same Way

Just behind the eyes sits the part of the brain that weighs options, tracks what each is worth, and ties feeling to choice — the ventromedial prefrontal and orbitofrontal cortex. When it decides between two things you might want, it appears to work the way essay 2's perceptual decisions did: evidence piles up for each option, the options suppress each other, and one of them settles.

Three lines of evidence converge on this.

**Recording.** Erin Rich and Jonathan Wallis trained monkeys to choose between two cues worth different rewards, recording from orbitofrontal cortex as they decided. Decoding the pattern across the recorded neurons moment by moment, they watched the activity swing between the two values before settling on one — what you'd expect if competing groups of neurons were suppressing one another and tipping toward a winner, not if a fixed value were being read off and acted on.[^rich-wallis] It's the pattern essay 2 found in LIP, the patch of parietal cortex where what the eyes take in becomes where they'll look next: graded competition resolving into commitment.

**Modeling.** Laurence Hunt and colleagues built attractor-network models of value-based choice and asked whether they predicted how brain signals changed over time while people made similar decisions. They did. Easy choices settled fast, hard ones slowly, as a model with mutual inhibition predicts.[^hunt-2012]

**Intervention.** The first two watch; this one reaches in. Run a weak current over medial frontal cortex — transcranial direct-current stimulation, a milliamp or two you'd barely register as a tingle — while people pick between options of differing value, and their choices get noisier: they take the better option less often. That's what an attractor model of the choice predicted. Raise the resting activity of its competing groups of neurons, as the current is thought to do, and random fluctuation from before the options even appear starts tipping the contest. The same current over lateral prefrontal cortex changed nothing.[^vmpfc-attractor]


No one of these is decisive alone. Three methods pointing the same way is harder to explain away than any one of them.[^three-methods] Choosing what you want and deciding what you see settle the same way.

Every study above is about *value* — which cue, which gamble, which reward — and none of them is about moral judgment. They show that the part of the brain that weighs worth and settles on a choice has the shape we've been tracing. What makes that bear on morality is anatomy: a white-matter highway runs from the amygdala to these same prefrontal regions,[^uncinate] so the emotion circuitry from the previous section and the valuation circuitry in this one are exchanging information within one connected system. On this evidence, moral judgment most likely runs on this, not on a separate module sitting somewhere else, waiting to be found.

### Why It Doesn't Reset the Way It Fired


Emotions are often easier to turn on than off. A sudden ambiguous noise can throw the amygdala and defensive systems into high alert quickly, but the body doesn't return to baseline at the same speed. The threshold for activation is low; the threshold for reset is higher. Safety has to accumulate.

Aggression shows a related pattern. Work from David Anderson's lab on circuits in the ventromedial hypothalamus suggests that aggressive internal states can persist and ramp rather than simply snapping on and off — the kind of dynamics you'd expect from an attractor stretched along a range rather than a point.[^aggression-line-attractor] A state like that can rest nearly anywhere along the range, at any level of aggression, instead of sliding back to zero when whatever set it off is gone. Once an emotional state has settled, leaving it can cost more than entering it did.

Fear conditioning shows the same basic logic from another direction. Go back to LeDoux's rats, freezing at a tone that used to come with a shock. Stop the shocks and the fear fades, but the original learning hasn't simply been erased. Extinction is closer to learning a second lesson: this sound is now safe here, in this context. That's why fear can return after time, stress, or a change of place.[^fear-conditioning] The circuit has a history. It doesn't just read what's in front of it and respond in proportion.

You can watch the same asymmetry in human perception directly. Show someone a face that morphs frame by frame from happy to afraid and ask at each step which emotion it is. The switch point isn't fixed — it depends on which end they started from. Coming from "happy," they hold onto "happy" well past the midpoint; coming from "afraid," they hold onto "afraid" just as stubbornly. The percept persists against the incoming evidence. It's the hysteresis essay 1 found in the switch between hearing "say" and hearing "stay," now in affect.[^emotion-hysteresis]

So the same handful of processes keeps reappearing in the circuitry of feeling: associations written in by experience, options competing and settling, states that linger once they're on, transitions that depend on the path in. Moral sentiment looks basin-shaped because the wiring underneath has the same features we've already seen elsewhere.

This could be wrong. It fails if emotional learning turns out to work differently from cortex: a non-Hebbian rule, settling without competing groups of neurons suppressing one another, or transitions that don't depend on the way in. Some of that may yet turn up. So far the evidence runs the other way.

If moral sentiment is basin-shaped, carved by learning, two questions follow: what does that tell us about sentiment, and what does it tell us about morality?

Some of the answer is already in the wiring. Sentiment isn't a binary stamp on an action; it's a pull whose strength depends on where the action lands: in which valley, how deep it is, and how steeply the ground falls toward it. Approval and disapproval come in degrees, and where an act sits shifts with context.

Take an ordinary case and walk it through. Someone lies. If it's a stranger inventing a reason to end a phone call, the pull is barely there — the act sits far up a shallow slope, and you register it and move on. Make the liar a friend and the lie about something that mattered, and the act drops into a deep, steep valley: the disapproval arrives fast, whole, and with force you didn't assemble. Now change one thing — the friend was lying to protect someone else — and you can feel the act move while you watch. Its description hasn't changed. What shifted is where it sits in the terrain, and the feeling shifted with it, before you had finished reasoning about it. That's what it means to say the wrongness is a gradient rather than a stamp: not that you're unsure, but that the strength of the pull depends on where the act lands, and where it lands moves with context.

Every witnessed act of kindness or cruelty, every reward or punishment absorbed, every story heard, every direct experience of harm or care is a training example, and the landscape gets carved a little deeper or a little shallower each time. On the Humean picture I'm using here, the wrongness you feel isn't a label read off the act. It's the gravity of a basin that learning has carved.

But that just tells us what moral feeling is. It doesn't tell us where the basins come from. At least three processes carve them, on very different timescales: evolution, slowest of all, then culture, then personal experience.

## What Carves the Basins

Vampire bats regurgitate blood to colony-mates who haven't fed that night, and the recipients preferentially return the favor to the bats that fed them.[^vampire-bats] That's reciprocity in mammals — you scratch my back, I scratch yours — the logic game theorists later wrote down, most famously as tit-for-tat. And when they wrote it down, it wasn't derived from first principles: tit-for-tat was discovered empirically, winning a pair of computer tournaments around 1980 in which strategies played a repeated game of cooperate-or-betray (the Prisoner's Dilemma), and only then shown to be stable, as long as the same partners are likely to meet again.[^tft-stable] Nature had been running reciprocity for millions of years before the theory caught up.

That's the slowest of the three carving processes at work. The selection pressure for reciprocity isn't a feature of human culture. It's a feature of being a long-lived social animal that has to survive many lean nights. Across millions of years, selection pressure on social mammals has carved deep grooves into the emotional systems: basins for cooperation, for reciprocity, for harm-avoidance, for care of offspring. These grooves are older than human cognition, laid down before there were humans to feel them.

Selection can't hand down a judgment, though, so it's fair to ask how it reaches the basins at all. Hebbian learning and natural selection aren't the same process: Hebbian learning changes synaptic weights within a lifetime, while natural selection changes the inherited processes that build and tune the nervous system, across generations. But selection can shape the landscape the learning happens on. If a certain pattern of response helped organisms survive and reproduce — care for offspring, aversion to harm, sensitivity to betrayal, readiness for reciprocity — then organisms whose nervous systems learned and retained that response more easily would tend to leave more descendants. Later generations wouldn't inherit the learned judgment itself. They'd inherit a nervous system biased toward forming that kind of judgment: a landscape in which some valleys are easier to carve, easier to enter, and harder to leave.

The same pressure shows up when you compare moral life across human cultures. Cultures vary enormously in their food taboos, sexual norms, honor codes, and in who counts as a person whose interests matter. What varies much less is the treatment of those already inside the circle: unprovoked harm to in-group members, abandoning one's own children, and betraying partners who have kept faith with you are close to universally condemned, even where the boundary of the in-group is drawn very differently.[^moral-universals] Cultures converge on the *kind of act* condemned, but not on who is protected by the rule — which is what you'd expect if selection carved the basin and culture sets its edges.

The Ultimatum Game studies of Henrich and colleagues, run across fifteen small-scale societies, make the pattern concrete. In the game, one person proposes how to split a sum of money. The other person can accept the offer, or reject it so both players get nothing. A purely self-interested chooser should accept any nonzero offer. But no population matched that textbook prediction. The pattern of deviation was uneven. Some societies showed comparatively low rejection rates; others rejected even hyper-fair offers. But the fact that people repeatedly acted against narrow self-interest, and would pay a personal cost to enforce some local idea of fairness, was widespread.[^ultimatum] What's universal isn't the exact line but the fact that there's a basin for fairness at all; how deep it is and what shape it takes vary. Fairness isn't on that short list of near-universal prohibitions, but it follows the same split: the disposition is everywhere, and each culture sets where it bites.

The other two carving processes work fast enough to see in a single lifetime, and they work the same way: ordinary Hebbian learning, the kind we've been tracing since essay 1, carving whatever the environment happens to feed it.

Culture supplies one stream. Stories about who counts as a hero or a villain, laws about what gets punished, religious narratives about which acts the universe holds against you, the pattern of who gets honored at the dinner table and who doesn't: these are repeated, structured inputs. The basins they carve can become deep enough that, by adulthood, they feel less like inheritances than like discoveries about how the world is.

Slavery shows how much this matters. Read through this picture, it's learning working normally and producing a monstrous result. For centuries it was morally invisible in many of the societies that practiced it — not defended against objection so much as simply not seen as requiring one. The basins for in-group cooperation were entirely intact in those societies; people were scrupulous, loyal, and generous within the circle. What had been carved shallow, or carved against, was the basin for out-group inclusion, and where the circle's edge fell was cultural training rather than anything built into the brain.

Which is why the change, when it came, didn't come from an argument that hadn't been available before. The arguments had been available for a long time. What moved was the training data: testimony from the enslaved, the slave narratives that circulated in enormous numbers, contact and proximity, the accumulating first-person accounts that made it progressively harder to treat a person as outside the circle. Abolition took generations, ran through politics and war as well as persuasion, and the moral shift ran alongside all of it. But on this picture the shift itself ran the way basins change: not new premises but new inputs, reshaping which basin an act falls into. What changed was what got carved, not how carving works.

Personal experience supplies the other stream, through the same learning, and it can carve as deep as culture does. A single severe childhood injustice can carve a basin for unfairness deep enough to organize a lifetime's moral reactions around it. Knowing someone who has been seriously wronged often shifts moral judgments more than any argument about the wronged class can.

Through the 1990s and 2000s, Americans who personally knew someone gay were markedly more supportive of gay marriage, and more and more people did.[^contact-effect] Personal experience writes into the same circuitry that culture writes into, using the same rule. I find it amazing these are the same process.

That's the full layered picture: evolution beneath culture beneath personal experience, each carving on top of the last. The deep basins are stable across cultures because they were carved by selection pressures that operated on every human population. The shallower basins vary because the inputs vary. All three carve the same landscape, by different routes: selection by shaping what each brain finds easy to learn, culture and experience by what they give it to learn.

What kind of moral picture does that leave? One in which moral life is neither arbitrary nor logically necessary. It isn't arbitrary, because the inputs are real and so are the limits they set. It isn't necessary either, because the basins depend on the human condition staying roughly as it has been.

The closest existing tradition is Aristotelian — and I'll admit I didn't expect to end up there. I came into this expecting the biology to dissolve the old moral theories, not to recover one of them, even in part. That's where this is going. First, though, the two traditions that dominate modern moral philosophy: utilitarianism, which judges an act by its consequences for everyone's welfare, and deontology, which judges it by duties and rules. Both try to put the deciding somewhere tidier than feeling.

## The Cliffs of Moral Theory

Both traditions present moral judgment as if the deciding happens in arithmetic, in logic, in universal law, in the consistent application of a rule. But a person still has to accept the verdict, and accepting it runs through the same landscape as everything else. We accept or resist the conclusion by how it lands in the landscape. Whether utilitarianism or deontology is true is a separate question; the question here is how any verdict comes to be accepted or resisted.

Hume would push further. The decision to let arithmetic be the arbiter isn't itself delivered by arithmetic. The decision to let universal law be the arbiter isn't itself delivered by universal law. Somewhere upstream, we have to decide that adding up everyone's welfare impartially, consistency, rights, dignity, care, or some other value is the thing whose pull should organize the rest. On the Humean picture, that decision already belongs to sentiment. The theory may formalize the route, but sentiment chooses which route feels authoritative.

Both traditions build sharp edges into what morality asks of us — a place where the obligation flips over, all at once, from not-required to required. Utilitarianism puts the edge in the arithmetic: once the numbers favour one action, that action becomes obligatory. Five lives outweigh one. A distant stranger's suffering counts as much as the suffering of someone in front of me. A small sacrifice here becomes mandatory if the total benefit elsewhere is large enough.

Deontology puts the edge in the logic: universalizable or not — could everyone consistently act on this rule? — permissible or forbidden, using a person merely as a means or not. One edge is mathematical, the other conceptual. Both ask a mind whose judgments are graded, felt and shaped by history to judge as though moral space came already divided. The way these judgments are actually learned gives us reason to expect friction right there.

And you can see the friction in the theories themselves. Each tradition has softer versions, and they exist partly *because* the simple ones demand more all-or-nothing judgment than people will actually go along with.[^c3-refinements] They're attempts to repair that mismatch: graded feeling forced through a theory that assumes clean divisions.

That's why the cliffs never remain purely theoretical. We judge the utilitarian conclusion by whether the sacrifice it demands feels noble, monstrous, compulsory, or insane. We judge the deontological conclusion by whether the rule feels principled, rigid, evasive, or sacred. The theory may supply the rule or the calculation, but sentiment supplies the gravity that makes the answer feel acceptable or intolerable. What learning produces is local, vivid, partial, and shaped by training. It cares more easily about the child in front of us than the statistical stranger far away. It treats physical contact, intention, proximity, and social meaning as morally weighty, even when a theory wants to abstract them away.

Kant is the clearest case of the logical cliff, and the natural one to set against Hume, because his project was in one sense the opposite of Hume's. Where Hume grounded morality in feeling, Kant grounded it in a single formal test: act only according to a principle you could will to be a universal law. He called this the *categorical imperative*. Morality, for Kant, isn't about how you feel. It's about what reason demands. A moral rule must be universalizable, non-contradictory, and applicable regardless of your sentiments about it. Its pull is easy to feel all the same: there are things you may not do to a person, however much good doing them would buy, because a person isn't something you spend.

Kant, in other words, tried to impose a cliff — and by now the pattern should look familiar. Twice before, a tidy theory has described a different mind from the one learning builds. Analytic philosophy wanted necessary and sufficient conditions for concepts whose boundaries slope. The ideal for confidence wanted a degree of belief about every question, all consistent with one another and indifferent to the order the evidence came in. It wanted that from a mind whose settled states are sparse, local and shaped by their own history. Now moral theory wants sharp obligation from graded, overlapping, context-sensitive sentiments. Each time, the theory describes the reasoner we hold up as the standard. The mind we have was built some other way.

The result, I think, is the same: paradoxes that won't go away, at the places where the theory and the mind we have part company.

Consider the trolley problem, or rather, the family of trolley problems, because what makes them interesting is the pattern across the variations. A runaway trolley will kill five people. You can divert it to a side track where it will kill one. Most people say: divert. Now change the scenario. You're on a bridge. You can push a large man onto the tracks, stopping the trolley but killing him. Same arithmetic, one death to save five. But far fewer people will push. And most can't give a reason that holds up.

Deontology has to find a principled difference between the two cases. "Don't use a person as a means" handles the bridge case but creates problems elsewhere. Is diverting the trolley also using the one person as a means? Is quarantining someone during a pandemic? Every proposed principle either draws the line in the wrong place for some variant or generates new contradictions when extended. Decades of moral philosophy have produced increasingly elaborate rule systems to handle an expanding set of trolley variants, and none has won general agreement.

The geometry suggests a different diagnosis. "Do not kill" and "save lives" are two deep basins. The trolley problem plants the marble on the ridge between them: it's built to sit where the pull from both is roughly equal, so that a small change sends judgment either way. It's the moral version of the saddle where essay 1 found the heap paradox.

The divert case puts the marble slightly closer to "save lives": the killing is indirect, impersonal, a side effect. The push case puts the marble slightly closer to "do not kill": the killing is direct, physical, personal. The agonizing quality of the dilemma *is* the experience of the marble sitting near the saddle.

Try it on yourself. Decide you wouldn't push the man, and hold the decision. The case doesn't go quiet. The five are still on the track, and you can feel the pull toward them that you've just overridden; you've come down on one side of the ridge, and the other valley hasn't stopped pulling.[^moral-remainder] That leftover discomfort is what a principle can't account for. A principle, once you've found it, should make the case easy, the way the right rule makes a sum easy. A ridge between two deep valleys stays a ridge after you've stepped off it.

The same geometry says people won't all step off on the same side. Basins carved by different moral experience have slightly different shapes, so the saddle sits in a slightly different place for each person, and the closer a case is built to the ridge, the more people should split. That's the pattern in the largest study of these dilemmas, seventy thousand people in forty-two countries. About four in five would divert the trolley; about half would push the man.[^trolley-42] The order held in every country. The levels didn't.

## Aristotle, Re-Read

One tradition in moral philosophy came close to this picture without access to the biological research or mathematics.

Aristotle's account of how a person becomes virtuous is short and concrete. We become builders by building, lyre-players by playing the lyre, just by doing just acts, brave by doing brave acts.[^nic-eth-ii] Virtue, for him, is a *hexis*: a settled disposition acquired through repeated action, eventually becoming the place a person naturally ends up. You don't reach courage by learning a principle about courage. You reach it by practicing courageous behavior until courage is where you settle.

That is very close to a description of what Hebbian learning does to a basin. Repeated co-activation strengthens the synapses linking the relevant cells, and with enough repetition the basin gets deep enough that the response becomes the default, the valley floor you settle into without deliberating. Aristotle didn't have the mathematics for it, but he had what it's like from the inside exactly right. Virtue is trained stability.

Two other parts of Aristotle's account fit the picture. Practical wisdom (*phronesis*), the trained sensitivity to what a particular situation calls for, is attunement to the local slope: sensing where this situation sits in the landscape and which way the ground tilts from there, picked up through the same experience that carved the basins to begin with. It doesn't tell you which valleys to aim for. That prior question, which ends are worth pursuing at all, belongs to the *telos*, Aristotle's word for the end or function a human being has. Practical wisdom is what you use once you're inside the landscape.

And the "thick" moral concepts that Philippa Foot and Elizabeth Anscombe wrote about, words like *cruel*, *courageous*, and *generous* (terms that are descriptive and evaluative at once), are what you'd expect basins to look like as they appear in ordinary moral language.[^foot-anscombe] Cruelty isn't a neutral description that then gets a moral label attached to it. It's a single basin, where recognizing the act and condemning it happen as one event. That would explain why moral reactions are fast, and why attempts to separate the descriptive content of moral terms from their evaluative content often feel forced.

That leaves the question of where Aristotle's obligation comes from, because a settled disposition alone can't explain why that disposition is good. You can ask: why aim for the courageous basin rather than the cowardly one? Aristotle's answer isn't a rule (Kant) or a calculation (Mill). It's a claim about what kind of creature we are.

Humans have a *telos*, and it defines what flourishing looks like. The virtues are the character states that let a human being fulfill that nature. The *ought* is grounded in the *is* of what kind of creature you happen to be. A knife ought to be sharp because cutting is what knives are for. A human ought to be courageous, just, and temperate because reason and social life are what humans are for.

Aristotle thought the telos was metaphysically fixed, part of the eternal essence of the species. We don't need to follow him there. Swap his fixed essence for evolved human nature — deep evolutionary attractors in place of an eternal purpose — and watch what happens to the argument on a single basin.

Take harm-avoidance. On Aristotle's telling, a human recoils from cruelty because avoiding wanton harm is part of what a well-functioning human *is*, and that essence tells you that you *ought* to recoil. Now make the swap. The recoil is still there, and its source is still human nature — but "human nature" now means a basin that selection carved because organisms that flinched from harm left more descendants. Notice what carried over and what didn't. The basin carried over: we really do have this disposition, and it really is a consequence of the kind of organism we are, not a cultural accident. What fell off is the last step. Selection explains why the basin is *there*; it says nothing about whether you *ought* to obey it. The recoil was selected for its fitness payoff, and a fitness payoff is an outcome, not a purpose.

So this much survives the swap: humans have a nature, that nature is one source of the deep moral basins, and the basins for reciprocity, harm-avoidance, care for offspring, and in-group fairness aren't arbitrary cultural products but consequences of the kind of organism we are.

The keystone doesn't. Aristotle used the telos to explain not only how humans tend to live but how they ought to live, and evolution, as the harm case just showed, supplies outcomes rather than purposes. So the substitution preserves most of Aristotle's description and removes the source of the obligation. "The kind of creature we are" can describe the basins; it can't, by itself, license the move from "we are this kind of creature" to "we ought to live the way this kind of creature flourishes." That piece doesn't translate. It's missing.

What the geometry does deliver is the shape of the moral landscape you're actually navigating: which basins exist, how deep they are, how they got carved. That's worth having, but it's not a grounding.

It's also less comfortable than it sounds, because evolution didn't carve only the basins we'd want to call virtuous.

The same selection pressures that produced reciprocity also produced in-group favoritism. The same pressures that produced care for offspring produced aggression toward perceived threats. Dominance hierarchies, xenophobia, status competition: these are deep evolutionary basins too, carved by pressures just as ancient and just as robust. The geometry explains the *shape* of all of them equally well. It doesn't pick favorites. If you ask "why should I aim for the reciprocity basin rather than the dominance basin?" the geometry has no answer. The valleys are just valleys. The gravity is just gravity. A basin's depth doesn't make it good, and its evolutionary age doesn't make it right. If you want to argue that cooperation, care, and reciprocity are better than aggression and dominance — and I do — you need grounds the geometry itself can't provide.

Still, put Hume and Aristotle together through evolutionary biology and the descriptive pieces fit. Hume told us that sentiment creates moral gravity, not reason. Aristotle told us that we're built by nature to acquire the virtues, and that practice finishes the job. The sentiments Hume observed aren't free-floating feelings that could have gone any direction. They're the felt experience of navigating a landscape that evolution shaped and experience carved.

The virtue tradition had the right picture of what moral judgment feels like from the inside, and the right instinct that any ethics has to be built from the material human nature supplies. What it lacked was an account of why trained dispositions take the shape they do, and an explanation of why human nature is stable enough to build on without treating it as an eternal essence. The geometry can help with the first. Evolutionary biology can help with the second. Neither supplies the grounding; that has to come from somewhere else. The picture that comes out is Aristotelian without being Aristotle's.

## Doesn't This Make Morality Arbitrary?

If moral basins are carved by inputs that vary, this starts to sound like relativism, and the sharpest version of the worry is easy to state: on this picture there's no ground to say one culture's morality is better than another's. Slavery was wrong for us because our basins say so; for a culture whose basins endorsed it, it wasn't.


Yes, on this picture morality depends on the human condition: on evolutionary inheritance, on social structures, on the specific landscape that biology, culture, and personal experience have carved. But that doesn't mean anything goes. Reciprocity isn't a coin flip; it's a pattern that emerges wherever social animals depend on continued cooperation. Harm-avoidance isn't an evolutionary accident; organisms that don't avoid harm don't persist, and social animals that harm their own don't keep a group. The deep basins converge across cultures for the reasons the carving picture gave; the shallower ones vary because the inputs vary.[^depth-metaphor]

That answer doesn't reach the slavery case, though. Slavery didn't sit in some shallow basin of its own. The basins against harm and betrayal were intact in the societies that practised it. What culture set was where the circle's edge fell: who counted as inside. So the relativist's question is really about the edge. Was drawing it there wrong, or only wrong by our lights?

When I call slavery monstrous[^c3-quasi], I'm speaking from inside my own basins,  I can't pretend otherwise. In 1903 G. E. Moore devised a simple test for any theory that tries to define "good" as some natural property: whatever property you name — pleasure, fitness, social utility, the pull of an attractor basin — you can always still ask, "But is *that* good?" and the question stays open rather than answering itself. A deep valley of disapproval toward cruelty is a fact about the landscape: it tells you that you'll reliably feel disapproval when you meet cruelty. It doesn't tell you that you *should*, or that cruelty is really wrong independently of human minds and responses. The gravity is the *is*; the *ought* is a different question, and the geometry can't answer it.

## Four Bets This Picture Makes

The picture makes four predictions about moral judgment, and each follows from the shape of the landscape.

**Moral dilemmas cluster at saddle points between strong sentiments.** They aren't scattered at random. The dilemmas that produce the most disagreement and the most distress are the cases where two deep basins compete, where the marble sits on a ridge with strong gravity on both sides. That's only a bet if the ridge can be found apart from the disagreement it's meant to explain: measure the pull of each side on its own, how strongly people hold "do not kill" and "save lives" separately, and check that the cases where both are strong are the ones that split people.

The trolley case plants the marble on exactly such a ridge, and other cases sit on similar ones: autonomy versus harm prevention in arguments over euthanasia or drug policy, fairness versus loyalty in whistleblowing, individual rights versus collective welfare in quarantine and taxation. Their difficulty is a property of the geometry — the flatness of the ridge — and easy moral cases are easy because the marble sits deep in a single basin with no competing pull.

**How firmly a moral judgment is held doesn't track the reasons a person can give.** The moral judgments people hold most firmly are often the ones they can articulate least well. The wrongness of harming children, the incest taboo, the gut-level reaction to betrayal: these are deep, steep-walled basins carved by evolutionary pressure, and people are immovable on them despite being unable to say why.

At the other end, the moral questions people hold most loosely, optimal tax policy, the ethics of gene editing, obligations to future generations, are the ones they can reason about most fluently. If moral judgment ran on stated reasons, we should expect the opposite pattern. You should hold most firmly the things you can argue for best. The geometry predicts the reverse, and the dumbfounding cases at the start of this essay point the same way. Haidt's intuitionism expects the same pattern; what the geometry adds is that the pattern falls out of the shape without being put there.

It's tempting to hear that as saying that a judgment you can't explain is evidence of a deeper moral truth. It doesn't say that. It says the inability to articulate doesn't by itself show the judgment is unreliable; it's a sign the basin is old and steep. Whether it should be that deep is a separate question, and essay 5 takes up why the convictions we hold most deeply are the hardest to give grounds for.

**Moral persuasion changes the landscape rather than proving a conclusion.** What changes people's moral judgments are narratives, direct experience, empathy exercises, exposure to suffering they had been ignoring, meeting someone from a group they had been abstractly judging. These work because they supply new experience, and the experience reshapes the basin.

Step-by-step argument rarely works by itself. Peter Singer's case for extreme altruism is the standing example: the premises are explicit and the conclusion follows,[^singer-1972] and very few people, even among those who find the argument forceful, live as if they accept it.[^charitable-giving] The geometric reason is that there's a deep, evolutionarily ancient "care for kin and near others" basin and a shallower, more recent "care for distant strangers" basin, and Singer's argument tries to move the marble between them using logic alone. It fails because validity can show that a conclusion follows; it can't make the premises feel binding. Reason navigates the terrain; it doesn't create the gravity.

None of this says Singer is wrong. The geometry explains why his argument often fails to move people, which is a claim about moral psychology, not about whether the argument is correct. Singer might be right that distance is morally irrelevant; the geometry can't settle that.

**Hysteresis in moral judgment.** Essay 1 found the pattern in "say" and "stay": once a network settles into a basin, the path out is not the path in. Essay 2 found it again in "ba" and "pa," and in the students who kept a verdict on their own ability after learning that the feedback behind it was made up. Earlier in this essay it showed up in the faces morphing between happy and afraid. The geometry predicts the same pattern in moral judgment itself.

Take an ambiguous moral case that can be framed as a harm case or as a fairness case. Present it to one group with the harm framing first, then gradually change the details until the case looks more like fairness, asking at each step which kind of wrong it is. Present the same case to another group with the fairness framing first, then gradually change it toward harm. If people sorted the case by a single fixed rule, the switching point would be the same in both directions. If they're settling into basins, it won't be: whichever kind of wrong they started with will hold on past the midpoint, the way "happy" held on in the morphing faces.[^moral-order] A rule could be given the same lag, the way a thermostat is given two set points, but it would have to be added by hand. Settling into basins can't avoid it.

## Where This Leaves Us

We started with a working assumption: that moral judgment starts as sentiment, and reasoning comes after. From there we asked what looking at the wiring underneath sentiment tells us about morality. The wiring turned out to work the way the previous essays found it working, and the moral landscape that comes out looks remarkably like what Aristotle described, minus the metaphysical commitments he wanted to give it.

If something like this picture is right, then moral life looks the way learning carves it: stable where the basins are deep, variable where the basins vary, training-sensitive throughout. That's a description, not a defense.

Whether moral life *is* "real" in some stronger metaphysical sense — whether the basins track moral facts or merely produce moral judgments — is a question the geometry doesn't settle. I've said where I come down, and the geometry neither forces that view nor rules it out. What the geometry does say is that the arguments people give for their moral positions aren't, in general, what's holding the positions in place. The basins are. The arguments come up to meet them. That's a claim about the psychology of moral conviction, and it can be accepted by people who hold very different views about whether moral judgments correspond to anything beyond human response. None of it delivers an ought; the question "but is that good?" stays open. What the geometry does is identify the kind of process moral judgment is, so that moral argument and reform can address how judgment actually works rather than how earlier theories assumed it worked.

The hysteresis prediction is what I'd most like to see tested directly. If it runs the way the geometry says it should, this picture would have passed a test it could have failed. If it doesn't, the picture has to change. Either way, the geometry is something you can run experiments against.
