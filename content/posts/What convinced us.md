## 1. After the obvious

The last essay ended with me handing someone a derivation when they had asked what convinced me. Every step of it holds, but I held all of it for months while the result still felt wrong. What convinced me was the reshaping that came after, and that path is gone.

We carry a picture of ourselves as reasoners who can account for what we believe: point to the evidence, retrace the steps, answer for the conclusion. None of us fully lives up to it, but still. It's what we reach for every time we ask someone to justify a claim, and it's the standard we feel ourselves falling short of when the reasons won't come. That picture is now under pressure. How much of it can we keep?

It helps to separate what's actually uncomfortable here, because it isn't the conclusions. An argument can be sound and convincing whatever first convinced me of where it ends. I can follow its steps and check each one. That's the easy part. The hard part is this: if the argument isn't what convinced me, something else did, and I can't see what it was. Maybe I'm missing something. And when we ask ourselves whether we're right, what we're really after is less a checked chain of logic than the sense of conviction at the end of it. That sense is what we've started to distrust.

So the reasons we report can be trusted the way they always could: we can follow them. The doubt is about whatever did the convincing.

And a belief that arrived by some opaque route can be worked over later. We can lay out its assumptions, test it against cases, hand it to people looking for the flaw, and end up holding it a second time, for reasons we can state. Sometimes that changes nothing in the belief and everything in our hold on it. So reasoning survives. What we lose is the view of how our convictions were formed, not the power to make them answer for themselves.

Philosophers reached a version of this long before the biology: whether a belief is justified doesn't depend on where it came from. They cut the question in two: set aside a belief's origins, and ask only what entitles anyone to hold it now. Sellars gave the cut its sharpest form. To call a state knowing, he wrote, isn't to give an empirical description of it but to place it where claims are justified and challenged, in "the logical space of reasons."[^sellars-sor] In our terms: where a basin was carved is one question, and what it answers to now is another.

But the cut doesn't reach our discomfort. Once you've taken in how a brain learns, you can't leave the cut there, because we don't only want to know that an argument is sound. We want to feel convinced, and that feeling is what we now doubt. Put it plainly. If what convinces us can be quite different from the argument we give, then when we explain ourselves, to others or to ourselves, we may be offering one thing while being moved by another. And when we try to convince someone else, we hand over the argument, not what convinced us. How a conviction was formed, how deeply and out of what, bears on how far we should trust it. And from the inside, formation doesn't announce itself. A skill you practised for years, the politics you grew up inside, the habits of your trade, the things your language makes easy to say and hard to doubt: each arrives as the plain shape of the world, as *of course*. Hard-won expertise and sheer inheritance feel exactly the same.

Here is the problem at its plainest. I can't stop using my convictions. I think with them every hour; they decide what I notice, what I bother to check, and what sounds like nonsense. I can't see how most of them were formed. So do I trust them?

Trusting none of them isn't an option. A mind that checked where each conviction came from before using it wouldn't get through breakfast, and the checking would itself run on convictions it hadn't checked. So the question is which ones to trust, and how far, and whether we can tell from the inside. Before asking that of one mind, look at what we do as groups, since nobody can see into anyone else's formation either.

## 2. What we built

Start with a laboratory notebook. It's bound so pages can't be slipped in or torn out, written in ink, dated, sometimes signed by a witness. It exists so that the grounds of a claim outlast the head that had them, in a form someone else can check and the writer can't quietly revise. Memory keeps the lesson and loses the episode; the notebook keeps the episode.

Almost everything we call rigor works the same way. Writing fixes a claim so it can be returned to and found wanting. A citation points past the writer to a source someone else can pull. A proof lays out the steps so each can be checked without trusting whoever wrote it. Calibrated instruments, controlled experiments, replication, peer review, the rules of evidence, the chain of custody: each takes some of the grounds a single mind would absorb and forget, and pins them down outside it, where others can check them against the world. Together they work like a prosthesis.[^prosthetic] They hold grounds open to view because the mind can't hold its own.

Science is the most demanding version, and not because scientists are exempt. A scientist's sense that a step is obvious is as trained as anyone's. What makes science work is that it has long acted as if the grounds really do go missing. It distrusts perception, so it builds instruments; it distrusts memory, so it keeps records; it distrusts private conviction, so it publishes its methods for people with reason to find the flaw; it distrusts a single success, so it asks for repetition. None of this lifts anyone out of formation. The methods were learned too, and they can be corrupted. But a practice can still find out, from within, whether it's opening the world up or only defending itself. Lakatos put it this way: every research programme shields some core commitments, and what matters is whether its adjustments keep producing new contact with the world or merely patch the theory to fit what has already been seen.[^lakatos]

That's how a group sorts what was earned from what was only absorbed. But all of it works by moving grounds outside the single mind. No committee can audit the conviction you're reasoning from right now, or tell you which of your own *of courses* to trust as you think. So the question comes back to each of us: can we tell, from the inside?

## 3. What if we don't need to see inside?

There's a reply to all this. You're asking too much, it says. Knowing was never about seeing how your beliefs were formed. It's about standing in the right relation to the world. If the process that produced a belief is reliable, if it tracks what it's about, then the belief is knowledge, and it doesn't matter that you can't see the process.[^reliabilism] Philosophers who hold this are called externalists, because what makes a belief knowledge can lie outside what the believer can see. In our terms: don't ask whether the basin is transparent. Ask whether it tracks.

It's an appealing answer, and it fits what we've seen. The economics still lets me predict, though I can't fully account for it. A radiologist's trained eye still finds the tumor a novice misses. If knowing is reliable tracking, then much of what we know without being able to say how is knowledge after all, and the last essay loses its sting.

But it doesn't give us back what we wanted. It saves the knowledge and leaves the knower out. What it really says is: you were never the idealized reasoner, the one who can account for every belief, so stop trying. Try taking that advice. The next time someone asks why you believe something, you'll give reasons. The next time you argue, you'll treat your grounds as yours to defend. We can't stop. It's like free will: you can be convinced you have none and still be unable to decide anything except as if you did.[^give-up] The language of reasons isn't a coat we can take off.

But this answer is too simple. Reliable isn't yes or no. A conviction can track the world closely or loosely.

Think of pouring something into a vessel. Pour water, and it takes the vessel's shape exactly, every curve. Pour large, oddly shaped stones, and they still fill it, but only roughly: you can make out the vessel's outline, along with gaps and edges of their own. Learning is like that. The world is the vessel, and what we learn takes its shape to the extent that the world pushes back on it. That's the externalist's best reason, and a good one: whatever carved our basins took its shape from contact with a world that pushed back, so they track something. It reaches out and gets corrected, and how closely it ends up fitting depends on how often, and how fast, the correction came. That decides whether a conviction is water or stones.

Weather forecasters are corrected every day, within a day. When an American forecaster says there's a 70 percent chance of rain, it rains on about 70 percent of those days.[^calibration] Experts making long-range political forecasts are corrected rarely, years late, and with plenty of room to explain the miss away. When Philip Tetlock scored thousands of their predictions over two decades, they did little better than chance, and the worst of them were the ones who reasoned from a single big theory of how politics works.[^tetlock] Both groups feel sure. Every conviction about how the world works is also a bet on what it will do next; forecasts are just the bets that get scored. Studies of expert intuition point the same way: it earns trust where situations repeat and feedback comes quickly, and much less elsewhere.[^kahneman-klein]

Reliability also flattens. Two convictions can be equally reliable and formed in completely different ways: one learned last year and open to argument, the other laid down in childhood and held in place by the people you belong to. Reliability says nothing about how deep each one goes, or what put it there.

Does that matter, if both are reliable? It does, because we can't test everything. There's too much, and we can't keep testing it. How deep a conviction sits and where it came from are often all we have to go on before we look.

## 4. How deep does it go

Depth is how much it would take to move you off a conviction. How much of it you can put into words is a separate question, but the two tend to go together.

Some basins are shallow: learned late, easy to state, resting on reasons you can list. You can set one aside for the length of an argument and pick it up again, like a rule you learned last year or a fact you could be talked out of tomorrow. Others are deep. They were laid down early, before you could weigh them, and they shape what you notice before you've decided anything. Challenging one feels less like reconsidering a claim than like losing your footing. Polanyi said that we can know more than we can tell,[^polanyi] and the deep basins are where that knowing lives: competence that works through us without ever passing through a sentence.

Polanyi took this much further. In *Personal Knowledge* he argued that if all knowing rests on skills and commitments we can't fully spell out, then the old ideal of knowledge justified all the way down, explicit and impersonal, has to go. Belief, not doubt, is where knowledge starts, and the knower's task is to own the commitment: to hold what we hold, responsibly, and say so.[^polanyi-pk] Even the checking we praised in science, he saw, runs on trained judgment no one can fully state. What we know now adds something to his picture. What we know without being able to say it has a history: it was laid down by learning, and learning can be studied. Once we know where the basins come from, we don't have to treat what we can't tell as a closed box, to be trusted or not. We can look into it: how deep it sits, and what pressed it down.

You can feel depth in how an objection lands. Against a shallow conviction, a counterargument arrives as a counterargument: you weigh it, and you might move. Against a deep one, the same counterargument feels like nonsense, not even wrong. Wittgenstein noticed that some certainties work less like beliefs than like the hinges a door turns on: they aren't things we've checked, but things that must stay put for any checking to happen.[^hinges] We can't doubt those without dissolving the ground the doubt would stand on.

Depth comes from more than one place. A chess master reads a board deeply because thousands of hours pressed the skill down past words. A certainty you grew up inside, a caste, a creed, a national story, is deep for another reason: it was installed early and is held in place by belonging, so giving it up means becoming a different person among different people. Gadamer argued that inherited prejudices aren't mere errors but the ground we stand on to understand anything at all.[^gadamer] He's right that we can't think without such ground. But being the ground we understand through doesn't show that a conviction tracks anything. Some deep convictions were earned, and some were only early, and at the bottom, from the inside, the two feel alike.

So the question has depth in it now: how deep a conviction sits, what pressed it down, and whether we can climb far enough out of it to set it beside an alternative.

## 5. What's left of answering for a belief

Externalism gives up on seeing inside. Internalism refuses to: whatever justifies a belief has to be something you can reach from the inside, so you can say, from your own point of view, what entitles you to it. Its pull is easy to feel. A belief you hold without being able to say why seems less like something you reasoned your way to than something that happened to you.

In its strong form internalism wanted everything: a belief counted only if you could lay out its whole justification. That didn't survive the last essay. But a smaller version does, and we can turn it to a different use, telling what we earned from what we only absorbed.[^internalism] Take any conviction and ask which part you can state, which part you can argue for rather than just feel, which part is habit you can no longer unpack, which part you inherited and never tested, and which part runs so deep that suspending it would dissolve the question. The answer isn't yes or no. It sorts one belief into layers, some lit and some dark, and the work is to find where the light stops.

We already do some of this sorting, without calling it that. Think of the last time you caught yourself thinking: I'm only saying this because I'm tired. Or: that's my upbringing talking. Or: that's confirmation bias; I want this too much to trust how fast I got here. In that moment you weren't giving a reason. You were naming a cause, something that moves beliefs whether or not it should, and checking your belief against it.

Notice where that vocabulary came from. Some of it is folk wisdom, but not all: confirmation bias is a laboratory finding, and much of how we now second-guess ourselves came out of psychology and biology. So looking inward is already partly empirical. Experiments told us what to look for, and better experiments can tell us more.

And the externalist's question was always empirical. Whether a conviction tracks the world is what experiments test: does the trained eye find more tumors than the novice's, does the forecast come true? If empirical work can help us read our own basins, and it plainly helps us check whether they track the world, then internalism and externalism stop looking like rival theories. They look like parts of one empirical project: finding out what a basin tracks, and how much of it we can say.

## 6. How far to trust it

Across these essays the same few questions about a basin keep coming back. How hard is it to move? How much of it can we say? What does it track, and how often did the world correct it? Where did it come from? What kind of thing moves it? Each looks like something that could, in principle, be measured. Depth has the firmest handle, because the biology that cost us our grounds in the last essay also suggests what could make one basin harder to move than another: more passes of consolidation run over it, and more of later thought built on it.[^cls-depth] The account fits the chess master. It doesn't yet explain depth held in place by belonging, and whether any of this can be measured is a question for the people who keep the instruments.[^quine]

Where a conviction came from is the question most easily abused. Knowing its history isn't knowing its worth: a history can vindicate as readily as it debunks.[^nietzsche][^williams] Where it came from tells you how far to trust it before you look, not whether it's true.

So, back to the question the group couldn't answer for us: which of my convictions do I trust, and how far? I think we already know the shape of the answer from somewhere else. Go back to confirmation bias. I can't watch it working; I know about it because psychologists measured it in other people. And knowing about it doesn't mean running an experiment every time evidence comes in. It means that when the evidence agrees with what I already thought, and feels especially welcome, I pause and look again. I don't distrust everything I believe. I've learned the conditions under which my sense of being right deserves a second look.

Convictions can be handled the same way. One formed where the world corrected me rarely and late, like the pundit's, is a cue to look again; one corrected constantly, like the forecaster's, can be leaned on. And there are questions I can put to a conviction while I'm using it. Can I state the rule? What would change my mind? Does it hold away from home? Where I can answer, I have something to stand on. Where I can't, I've found what I'm taking on trust, and what's worth checking. So can we tell from the inside? Partly, and with tools made outside.

The answer stays partial for a reason the bias research also found. Knowing about a bias doesn't reliably remove it. People see biases readily in others and rate themselves as less biased than average, even after the bias has been explained to them.[^blind-spot] The cue can tell me when to look again, but the looking, done from inside, is weak. That's why we built the notebook and the referee: where one mind can't check its own grounds, other people can.

Don't ask only whether the basin tracks. Ask how closely, how deep it was cut, and what it was cut into.

## 7. The new self-image

So we end with a different reasoner from the one we were reluctant to lose. That one was to be the transparent owner of his grounds, who turns inward and finds his reasons waiting. He isn't there. What can stand in his place is humbler and more honest: someone who meets their own *of course* not as the plain face of the world but as something with a depth, a history and a track record that can be read, and who knows the reading is never finished.

What the reading yields isn't a verdict of trust or distrust but a grade. Some convictions have earned confidence, because the world shaped them by correcting us and we can still find the marks: not the episodes, which are gone, but what they left, a rule we can state, a skill that carries over to cases it never saw. Some have earned suspicion, because what shaped them was inheritance, repetition, belonging, or having wanted them true. Most have earned neither yet. The work isn't to clear our obviousness or to convict it, but to hold it open long enough to tell which kind we're dealing with.

Several views that looked like rivals turn out to be looking at one thing. The externalists saw a basin from the side of what it tracks. The demand to answer for a belief, which Sellars put as placing it in the space of reasons, saw it from the side of what we can say for it. Wittgenstein's hinges and Gadamer's inherited prejudices saw depth from the bottom; Polanyi felt it from inside, as a competence that outruns words. The notebook and the citation are what we build because a basin can't be held open from within. Each had a hand on the same thing: a structure laid down by learning that carries the world without carrying its own history. And because it's one thing, it has a depth, which is something the science can begin to speak to.

The economics is still with me, still sound, still unexplained by the reasons I'd give for it. I never learned how it convinced me; that path is gone, and the science says it was always going to be. What has changed is smaller, and enough. I no longer take its obviousness as the place where questions stop. I take it as the place where one begins. That's what's left to offer the reasoner after opacity: not his old transparency handed back, but something to do in the dark. Turn and face the *of course*. Ask it where it came from. Don't ask it to come clear; ask how deep it goes, and how often the world has told it it was wrong.
