## The thing I admired about people who kept asking

"You can't trust anyone over thirty" is a ridiculous line that I've always loved.[^c2-weinberg] What I hear in it is admiration for the willingness of the young to change their minds.

That's my bias: I admire people who stay humble and flexible as they get older. I think it's also something we take to be necessary for a philosopher. Thinking about it led me to an odd question. If the ideal is to always live with some doubt, why don't we? Do we get tired? Stop caring? Pick up the habit from everyone around us? Is it a failure that more discipline could correct, or is closing questions something a mind has to do?

We hold up the mind that keeps every question open as an ideal, for ourselves and for research and philosophy, and none of us gets there. Keeping a question open doesn't mean having no view. It means holding the view more or less firmly and letting each new piece of evidence move it a little. If what stops us from doing that everywhere is built into how minds work, then finding it tells us something about believing itself. The question becomes how a mind can weigh evidence by degrees and still arrive at something it treats as settled, and why it would need to.

---

## Two things with one name

Whatever you're in while a question is still open, and whatever you're in once it has closed, we call by one name: *belief*. That's where the trouble starts, because the word is doing two jobs that don't fit together.

Some of what you believe comes with a dial on it. You're fairly sure the meeting is at three. Surer that the restaurant takes cards. Not at all sure it'll be quiet enough to talk. Evidence nudges these rather than flipping them.

And some of what you believe has no dial on it anywhere. You don't consult your confidence that your keys are on the counter before walking over to get them. You go and get them.

That's odd, isn't it? We call both belief, but they describe very different mental states. Philosophers grappling with this generally fall into one of three camps.

**Credence-first.** Confidence comes in degrees (philosophers call a degree of confidence a *credence*), and that's all there really is in there. Believing is a way of talking about it, usually about a degree that's high enough.

**Belief-first.** Belief is the real material, and it's yes or no: you take something to be so or you don't. Talk of degrees is talk about *what* you believe, not a report of a second kind of state inside you. On one version, being fairly sure it'll rain is simply believing something about the rain: that it's fairly likely.[^c2-belieffirst]

**Dualism.** There are two attitudes in there. You believe things and you're confident of things, and neither is built out of the other.

None of this is about knowledge. Belief is narrower: the state you're in when you take something to be so, whether you're right or wrong.[^c2-knowledge]

None of the three has won, and each has very good arguments. So let's take a close look at the best version of each, and then see what the brain has to say.

## The things you don't stop to question

Stop the next time you work something out and look at what you're working from. To decide when to leave for the station, you use the train leaving at eight, the traffic being what it usually is, your watch being right. Any of them could be wrong, and you'd check one if you were given a reason to. Until then you don't weigh them, you don't ask yourself how likely each one is, and you don't plan for the version of the evening where they're false. You use them.

Most of what you believe works like that. You could question almost any of it if someone gave you a reason, and hardly any of it gets questioned while you're using it. That's why philosophers keep expecting belief to have a yes-or-no character: much of the time, that's how we experience it, and it's where the belief-first camp starts.

Their way of putting it is that believing something makes it a starting point for your reasoning (a premise). You treat it as true until something comes along that overrides it, and meanwhile the alternatives drop out of view. Philosophers call a hold like that *defeasible*: it can be overturned, but only by something with enough force behind it.[^c2-premise][^c2-premise-scope][^c2-premise-debate]

I find all of that persuasive, which is why the next thing bothers me as much as it does. If believing really is a state you're either in or out of, there should be a moment you entered it, a door you walked through. Go looking for one, in your own case or anyone else's, and it isn't there.

## The door nobody can find

Pick something you'd assert without hedging and that you didn't always hold, and go looking for the day it changed. What you'll usually find is that you can date the period, sometimes the year, and that the closer you get to a moment the more it dissolves. No afternoon where you weighed it up and came down on a side, no conversation that did it, nothing you could point at and say: there, that's where I stopped asking. There are beliefs that arrive in an instant, as when you're told a friend got married. For most of what any of us holds, the transition is missing in a particular way. It isn't that the event is forgotten. It's that "the moment I decided" doesn't describe anything that happened: nothing about the process had a moment in it.

That missing moment is where the credence-first camp starts. Put it beside what evidence actually does to you. You're waiting for someone at seven. At five past you're not concerned. At quarter past you check your phone. By half past you've started composing a message you don't send, and somewhere after that you're ordering another drink and thinking about how long you'll give it. At which minute did you start believing he wasn't coming? There's no minute. But you were doing something the whole time, and what you were doing tracked how sure you were, continuously, without ever passing through a gate.

That's the credence-first case. Stated plainly, it's very hard to argue with. Confidence varies by degrees. Evidence moves it by degrees. And it shows in what you do all day: you leave for the airport at a time that encodes exactly how much you trust the traffic, and you'd leave five minutes earlier if the flight mattered more.

And how sure you need to be depends on what's riding on it.[^c2-stakes] It's Friday, and you drive past your bank on the way home. The queue is out the door, so you decide to come back tomorrow; you were there on a Saturday a fortnight ago and it was open. Ask yourself whether the bank is open on Saturdays and you'll say yes, flatly, no hedging.

Now change one thing. The cheque in your pocket has to clear by Monday or you lose the house. Nothing about the bank has changed and you haven't learned anything new. But now you hear yourself saying: well, banks do change their hours, I should probably check.

Same evidence, same person, same afternoon. What changed is what it costs to be wrong, and with it how sure you need to be before you'll say yes flatly. The flat yes was a level of confidence all along, high enough for an ordinary Friday and not high enough for the house.

None of this is the claim that we walk around with numbers in our heads. Nobody thinks that. It's the claim that whatever we do walk around with comes in degrees, and that our categorical talk, *believes*, *knows*, *is sure*, is a coarse description laid over it rather than a report of a second kind of state.

That still leaves the yes-or-no talk to account for. Most philosophers on this side weren't content to call "believe" loose shorthand for "about seventy per cent".[^c2-jeffrey-belief] They could see, as the belief-first camp did, that much of what we hold gets used as a flat yes, in what we reason from and in what we say, and they wanted an account of it.

The simplest account, and the earliest, is a bar. You believe something when your confidence in it is high enough (the threshold view, also called the Lockean thesis).[^c2-lockean] The bank case is the bar moving: the same confidence clears it on an ordinary Friday and falls short when the house is at stake. It's one way of being credence-first, not the whole camp, and most of the argument over the last forty years has been about where the bar sits and whether it moves.

The bar runs into an old difficulty, and it belongs to anyone who has written something long. A careful author checks every claim in her book and believes each one; that's why they're in it. She also expects, from experience, that the book contains at least one mistake somewhere, and says so in the preface.[^c2-preface]

Look at what she does and doesn't believe. She believes the first claim, and the second, and so on to the last. She doesn't believe that all of them together are true; she believes the opposite. Believing each claim isn't already believing that every claim is true at once, and her confidence tells her why. Each claim may be very likely, but with hundreds of them, the chance that every single one is right is small.

Each claim clears the bar and the book as a whole doesn't. If believing each claim meant believing them all together, she'd end up believing both that everything in the book is true and that something in it isn't. So to keep the bar, you have to give up the idea that believing each claim commits you to believing them all at once. It's a principle most of us would have signed without a second thought, and it has to go to save the bar.

## Two attitudes that usually agree

The third camp takes the double job of the word at face value. On the dualist view, believing something and being confident of it to some degree are two distinct attitudes, each as basic as the other, and neither is built out of the other. Most of the time they agree: you believe what you're fairly sure of, and you're fairly sure of what you believe. But two things that usually agree can still be two things.

It's an attractive position: each attitude gets to do the job it seems made for. Confidence does the weighing: it moves smoothly with evidence, and it's what you bet with. Belief does the settling: it's what you reason from, assert and plan on. The dualist doesn't have to explain away either half, and doesn't have to say which one is the real one.

Two cases show why dualists think the states can't be merged. The first comes from the law. A bus hits someone at night and drives off. The Blue Bus Company runs eight of every ten buses on that route, and there is no other evidence at all. Ask yourself whether you believe the company's bus did it. Most of us find we won't say so; "probably" is as far as we'll go. Now swap the statistics for an eyewitness who saw the bus and who, we know from testing, gets this sort of identification right about seven times in ten. The odds are lower, and "yes, it was their bus" comes much more easily.

The market share tells you about buses in general and says nothing about this bus. The witness, unreliable as she is, was looking at *this* bus. The question here isn't whether the company should pay; it's what you yourself are prepared to believe. Why the kind of evidence makes that difference is disputed.[^c2-buchak][^c2-barestat] Take it at face value, as most do, and it shows what the dualist needs: more confidence doesn't automatically put you in a better position to believe.

The second case is the author with the preface. The threshold view struggled with her; the dualist can take her as she is. Her outright beliefs, one per claim, and her estimate of how likely the whole book is to be free of errors are two kinds of attitude, with no reason to line up claim for claim, and neither has to be reduced to the other. It isn't the only reading, but it's a natural one, and a good part of why dualism has the defenders it does.

So each camp sees something true. The belief-first view sees the settling, the credence-first view sees the weighing, and the dualist sees that the two don't always line up, and declines to reduce either. Three answers, each able to say what the others can't, and no obvious way to choose between them.

## The question underneath all three

Set the three answers beside each other and they disagree about nearly everything: whether there's one attitude in there or two, which is made out of which, whether the graded thing is the raw material or what's left over once you stop hedging. What they don't disagree about is what there is to explain. Confidence comes in degrees. Some of what we hold gets treated as settled. And sometimes the two come apart, as they do in the bus case and in the preface.

Each camp takes one of those observations as its starting point and builds outward from it. That's a reasonable way to work, and it may even be the right one, since what a belief does could be part of what makes it a belief. But an account built outward from one observation has to fit the others in afterwards, and it leaves some things unexplained. Why do the graded and the settled turn up in the same claims? Why is there no moment when a question closes? Why does the way you came to a conclusion matter, and not only how confident it left you?

When two features vary independently, as confidence and outright belief do in the bus case and the preface, that doesn't by itself show there are two states. Steel can be hard and brittle: it resists a dent but cracks when struck. Copper is soft and tough: it dents easily and doesn't crack. Hardness and toughness come apart all the time, and nobody has gone looking for the two substances. But the metal only shows that one thing with two features is possible. It doesn't show that's what a belief is. Whether there's one attitude in there or two is as open after the analogy as before it.

So leave aside, for now, what a belief ultimately is, and ask something all three camps need answered: how can a mind weigh evidence by degrees and still arrive at something it treats as settled? That's a question about how those three observations come about, and it's one you can put to a brain.

Until recently, behaviour was all any of us had to go on. We notice ourselves reasoning from a claim without reopening it, we feel our confidence slide as the evening wears on, we find ourselves fairly sure of things we won't say we believe, and we conclude what the three camps concluded. Given only behaviour, those are the natural conclusions, and most of us hold some version of one of them about our own minds without ever having argued for it. Now there's another kind of evidence. We can watch a brain weigh evidence and settle a question, and ask how it does both.

Whatever the brain turns out to do, it won't refute any of the three positions, because each can be extended until it fits. If belief and confidence turn out to move together, a dualist can let the two attitudes be as tightly coupled as you like. If the way you came to a conclusion turns out to matter, a credence-first theorist can build a model in which it does. Those extensions are legitimate, and they show that "this account can explain that" is worth little on its own. With enough additions you can explain anything. So the useful question isn't which account can be made to fit. It's what the brain does with nothing added.

That's what makes the biology a good place to start. Nobody built a brain to have the properties the three camps argue about, so whatever it turns out to have, nobody put it there. And there's a standard to measure it against: a theory of how confidence ought to behave, which all three camps can accept.

## How confidence ought to behave

All three camps grant that confidence comes in degrees somewhere. The credence-first view says that's all there is. The dualist puts it beside belief. The belief-first view folds it into belief, as a belief about how likely something is. Wherever it lives, there's a theory of how it ought to behave, usually called Bayesianism, and it belongs to none of the camps.

The first thing it says is which degrees can sensibly be held together. Suppose you're sixty per cent sure the home team will win tonight and also sixty per cent sure the away team will. Each feels defensible on its own; you like both sides. Together they can't be right. Only one team can win, so your confidence in the two can't add up to more than the whole.

Writing confidence down as numbers lets you say exactly what's wrong instead of only feeling it. Work the idea through and you get the whole of probability theory, read as rules that confidence ought to obey. There's a result behind the rules, too. If your confidences break them, someone can offer you a set of bets, each fair by your own numbers, that together lose you money whichever way the game goes. He doesn't need to know anything about football. He only needs your numbers to add up to more than a hundred per cent.[^c2-dutchbook]

The rules also tell you how to move. When evidence arrives there's one way of updating on it that keeps everything in line (Bayes' rule): your old confidence goes in, the evidence goes in, the new confidence comes out, and nothing is left to taste. That turns "weigh the evidence" from advice into arithmetic. It was an extraordinary thing to find, and I don't think the enthusiasm it produced was misplaced.

The rule has a gap at the beginning. It takes you from one confidence to the next and says nothing about the first one. Ask what you should think about something you know nothing at all about, and the rules allow any answer. People often settle on even odds, and there are arguments for doing so, but they're extra principles laid on top of the mathematics, and the answer they give depends on how you divide up the possibilities.[^c2-indifference] Every Bayesian model starts from something it was handed.

The rule is also hard to follow. Here is a test for a disease that one person in a hundred has. If you have it, the test finds it nine times out of ten. If you don't, it says you do about nine times in a hundred. Your test comes back positive. How worried should you be?

Most people put it somewhere above seventy per cent. So did most of the physicians it was put to, which is the part that should give you pause.[^c2-basrate] The answer is about nine per cent. Take a thousand people: ten have the disease and nine of them test positive, while of the nine hundred and ninety who don't, about eighty-nine test positive anyway. Ninety-eight positive results, nine of them real. The false alarms swamp the true ones because there are so many more people available to be wrong about.

You can't get there by thinking harder about how reliable the test sounds. You get there by counting, and the counting is Bayes' rule. Almost nobody does it by feel, and it would be natural to conclude that whatever a brain does with evidence, it isn't this. That conclusion turns out to be too quick.

*Confidence comes in degrees* is about what's there. *Those degrees should obey the probability rules and update by Bayes' rule* is about what's good, and you can believe the first without the second. Almost none of the Bayesian theory was offered as a description of what's inside a head. It says how confidence *ought* to be structured and how it *ought* to change: what a reasoner would do who never made a mistake. Whether human brains carry anything like a probability distribution is a separate question, and the philosophers who built the theory were mostly not answering it.

## What it would cost to keep everything open

The ideal reasoner never closes a question. Everything he holds stays a degree, ready to move with the next piece of evidence: the open mind I started with, taken all the way. Philosophers have understood for a long time why no real mind can be like that. C. S. Peirce, writing in the 1870s, described doubt as an irritation that inquiry exists to end. Wittgenstein, in the last notes he wrote, said the questions we raise depend on some things being exempt from doubt, the way a door depends on its hinges.[^c2-hinges] Nobody can hold every thread open at once. It would take more computing than any mind or machine has, it would need a starting point the rules can't supply, and people trying to live together couldn't afford it.

Go back to deciding when to leave for the station, and this time try it the ideal way, without settling anything. The train might be on time, late or cancelled. The roads might be clear or jammed. Your watch might be right or slow. Each combination is its own version of the evening, and each has to be weighed against the others. Three questions with two answers each give eight versions. Thirty give more than a billion, and an ordinary decision rests on far more than thirty things you could, if you wanted to, doubt.

Weigh every combination of a few hundred of them and the count runs past the number of atoms in the universe. No computer that could ever be built would finish. There are shortcuts, and for problems of the right kind they work very well, but no known shortcut works for every problem.[^c2-complexity][^c2-vanrooij] That doesn't mean nothing probability-like is going on when you decide to leave at quarter to eight. It means that whatever is going on, it isn't laying out every possibility and weighing it.

And even with unlimited time, the ideal can't start from nothing. Its first confidences are handed to it from outside the rules, and nothing in the rules can check them. Perfect updating still rests on a starting point that was simply taken as given.

Nor can people living together keep everything open. A verdict, a contract, a published result, a diagnosis written in a chart: each is a question a group has agreed to treat as closed so that others can build on it. That's a fact about what we need from each other rather than about what's inside any one head, and a later essay takes it up.

This is the belief-first camp's strongest argument. A reasoner with limited time and room can't carry every possibility, so it needs a way to stop carrying most of them, and believing is that way. Once you believe the train leaves at eight, the versions where it doesn't drop out, and what's left is a problem you can actually think about. A belief settles a matter the way a decision settles what you'll do: you can plan on it without reopening it.[^c2-simplify][^c2-clutter]

A mind has to close questions. It can't hold every one open, and it doesn't. As far as that side takes it, that's the answer to the puzzle from the start: we settle because a mind that didn't couldn't function. That answers why a mind settles. It doesn't say how a mind that weighs evidence by degrees gets to something settled, or why nobody can find the moment it happens.

Bayesian cognitive scientists, the people who do ask what's inside a head, work in a particular order. They start from the ideal, work out what a perfect reasoner would conclude, and treat what people actually do as an approximation to it. They don't claim the brain runs the rule; as one of the field's leading figures puts it, "It seems implausible that people are explicitly computing Bayes' rule every time they face a problem of inductive inference."[^c2-bayescog] The ideal comes first, and the brain is measured against it. This series works the other way round: it starts from what a brain does and sees what that lines up with.

## What a learning system does with a question

The last essay left us with a landscape. That's where to start.[^c2-essay1] Neurons that fire together strengthen their connections, learning shapes those connections slowly, and the shaping leaves some patterns of activity able to hold themselves together while others fall apart the moment they form. The space of everything the network could be doing is terrain, the self-sustaining patterns are valleys in it, and the marble rolls until it settles. A concept is one of those valleys.

The terrain is not the left and right of a diagram. Record from hundreds of neurons at once, ask how many independent directions the activity actually moves in, and the answer is many, more than a picture can show, and also far fewer than there are cells. The activity runs on a surface through the space rather than filling it.[^c2-manifold] Which directions that surface has isn't a fact about neurons in general. It's a fact about what this particular network was trained on, the distinctions it had to draw over and over until drawing them became the cheapest thing it could do.

A settled state here, then, is not a number. It's a position in a space with many directions, and two states can be equally hard to leave while differing in everything else.

Is that terrain a picture only of what neurons do, or also of what *thinking* does? A run of experiments, each a step further from the senses, lets us watch.

When neuroscientists want to see a decision in real time, they use a beautifully simple setup. A monkey watches a screen of jittering dots. Most move randomly, but some fraction drift together in one direction, and the monkey's job is to say which way and report it with a flick of the eyes. Electrodes record from an area called LIP, in the parietal cortex, where what the eyes take in is turned into where they'll look next. The neurons don't sit quiet and then fire. They ramp, slowly, like a tank filling: the cells favouring the correct direction climb over hundreds of milliseconds while the ones favouring the wrong direction stay flat or drift down. When the climb reaches a certain height, the eyes move. More coherent dots make a steeper ramp and a faster answer; noisier dots make a shallower ramp and a slower one.[^c2-drift]

Look at what those neurons are adding up. Each moment of the display is a small piece of evidence, some of it favouring left and some favouring right. Bayes' rule says how much each piece should count: by how much more likely it would be if the dots were really going left than if they were going right. Give every piece the weight it deserves, keep a running total, and the total tells you exactly how far the evidence so far favours one direction over the other. The climbing activity in LIP behaves very much like that running total. That's the reading Joshua Gold and Michael Shadlen give it, and a later experiment, in which monkeys combined symbols that each carried a known amount of evidence, found the same neurons tracking the summed weights.[^c2-logLR]

And they stop. When the total reaches a set height the eyes move, and the weighing is over. One ramp, two things: a graded quantity that tracks the evidence the way probability says it should, and a commitment at the end of it.

The decision looks instantaneous. The process behind it took most of a second and moved continuously the whole way. The climbing is in the firing; in landscape terms, the evidence tilts the ground, and the marble rolls downhill toward one valley and away from the other. The flick of the eyes is the marble arriving. The commitment was the last thing that happened, not the first.

What carries over to beliefs is the shape of the process, not its speed: a quantity that tracks the evidence, and a settled state that falls out of it with no separate step where a verdict is issued. The first essay had both speeds in one example. You recognise Jennifer Aniston in a fraction of a second: see half her face and the activity rolls the rest of the way into place. The valley it rolls into took years of watching her to carve, and nothing in those years was the moment you came to know her face. A belief formed over weeks runs on the slow clock, on the same ground as the fast one, and a monkey taught a new category shows that ground being reshaped.

If that ramp only appeared in visual areas, this would be an essay about eyesight. Kim and Shadlen recorded from dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, the region we point at when we want to point at deliberation rather than sensation, during a version of the same task, and found the same signature: gradual climbing rather than switching, faster climbing on stronger evidence, the decision arriving as the climb reached its height.[^c2-pfc]

That still leaves the possibility that prefrontal cortex is doing perception with extra steps. So take a case where the thing being decided isn't in the picture at all. Freedman and colleagues built images that morph frame by frame from a cat into a dog, then drew a boundary themselves, declaring everything before some arbitrary frame a cat and everything after it a dog. They trained monkeys on that invented line and recorded from prefrontal cortex. What the neurons tracked was the category, not the picture: two images equally far apart on the screen produced sharply different responses if the boundary fell between them, and nearly identical responses if it didn't. Move the boundary, retrain the animal, and the neurons follow. Nothing about the images changed. What the cells respond to is a distinction that exists only because it was learned.[^c2-pfc]

That is about as close as we currently get to watching a brain hold a category the world did not hand it, and it's held in the same region where evidence was watched piling up for a decision.

One more property, and it bears on the third question from earlier, why the way you came to a conclusion matters. You can hear it for yourself. Say "ba" and then "pa" and pay attention to your mouth: the lips do the same thing both times. The difference is timing, how long after the lips part before the voice switches on. In "ba" the voice comes almost at once; in "pa" there's a delay of a few hundredths of a second. That delay is a dial. Synthesise a series of sounds with the delay creeping up in even steps and you have a continuous ramp from one sound to the other, with nothing in between but slightly different numbers.

Play the series to someone step by step and ask what they hear. Somewhere along the way they stop hearing "ba" and start hearing "pa." Where that happens depends on which end you started from. Run the series the other way and the switch lands somewhere else. The listener isn't consulting a fixed boundary; they're holding on to whatever they last heard, and it takes a little extra to dislodge them.[^c2-hysteresis] Whatever is doing the categorising has some memory of where it has been. It's the hysteresis we met in the first essay with "say" and "stay."

So: a system that weighs evidence much the way Bayes' rule says to, moves continuously, settles into states that hold, carries distinctions it was taught rather than given, and doesn't keep the boundary between them in one fixed place.

None of that was built to settle a philosophical argument. It's what the recordings show, and it would look the same if nobody had ever asked what a belief is. The question now is which of the things the three camps argue about turn up in it anyway.

## The half that looks like probability

Start with the ramp, because it already answers half the question. The neurons in LIP were adding up evidence much the way Bayes' rule says to, and nothing in the monkey was looking the rule up. Whatever the brain is doing when it weighs evidence, it lands close to what the rule prescribes.

That can sound at odds with the medical test, where most people, physicians included, got the arithmetic badly wrong. The two aren't the same task. The neurons weigh evidence of a kind the system has met countless times, in the form it naturally arrives in. The test asks for a calculation over figures stated in words, and when the same figures are given as counts of people, far more people get it right.[^c2-basrate]

The ramp isn't the only place this shows. Alexandre Pouget and his collaborators have spent years on how populations of neurons carry information. No single cell stands for a single thing; the information lives in the pattern across many cells firing at once.[^c2-pouget] Take the simplest version. A line on a screen, tilted at some angle, and a population of cells in visual cortex that respond to orientation. Each cell has an angle it likes best and fires less as the line moves away from it. Show a line tilted thirty degrees and you get a hump of activity: the cells that like thirty degrees firing hardest, their neighbours firing somewhat, the ones tuned to ninety nearly silent.

Where the hump peaks is the system's best guess about the angle. The hump's width carries something too: sharp and narrow when the line is crisp and well lit, broad and smeared when it's dim or brief. One pattern of firing, and you can read a best guess off it and a degree of certainty off it, without there being two codes, two populations, or anything keeping them in step.

What these populations carry a spread *over* is a stimulus: the angle of a line, the pitch of a tone. It's the system's uncertainty about something it's looking at, not about whether a claim is true.[^c2-pouget-scope] Reading the peak as a belief and the spread as a confidence would be several steps past what the recordings hand you, and I'm not taking them.

What it does establish is narrower. A single signal that carries a best guess and a degree of certainty together is something brains build; we've found one and can watch it work. So nothing forces the two jobs onto two separate states. Whether there are two anyway is a different question, and the recording doesn't answer it.

The same thing turns up when people build networks instead of recording from them. Take the simplest artificial neuron: it adds up what its inputs send it and passes on a squashed version of the total, somewhere between off and fully on. Networks of these units compute exact probabilities when their connections are set just right, and nobody has to set them by hand. Train such a network on examples, nudging its connections each time its answer is off, and its outputs come to approximate probabilities on their own. Teach it handwritten digits, and the number it gives for "3" on a smudged figure ends up close to how often figures like that really are threes. Make it commit to one answer, by letting the strongest output silence the rest, and it can be made to pick, across many tries, each answer about as often as that answer is likely to be right.[^c2-mcclelland]

And there's now one measurement of the landscape itself. In 2023 a group at the US National Institute of Mental Health recorded from the prefrontal cortex of two monkeys deciding whether to accept offers of reward that varied in size and in how long they'd have to wait for it. For very good and very bad offers the monkeys decided the same way every time; for middling ones they wavered. Reconstructing the landscape from the recorded activity, the researchers found that the valleys the activity settled into were steeper, harder to climb out of, for the offers the monkeys decided consistently.[^c2-wang] They read consistency as confidence, which is a reasonable reading and still an inference. It's one study, in monkeys, about choices between rewards rather than beliefs about the world. Within those limits, it's a measurement of how steep a valley in a brain is, and the steepness tracks how consistently the choice is made.

Put these together and you find a system that does roughly what the ideal describes without anyone having built the ideal in. The brain doesn't run Bayes' rule. Even so, the Bayesian vocabulary describes what it does remarkably well.[^c2-company]

## The half that feels settled

At the bottom of a valley, the ground is flat. Step a little to the left, a little to the right, and you're still at the bottom. Nothing tilts, and no direction leads anywhere lower. That is what settledness feels like from the inside. It isn't a switch thrown. It's something you don't reconsider while you're thinking, because every small move shows no way down.

That's why nobody can find the door. There was no moment to find, because the flatness isn't something you enter; it's what's left when the slope runs out. The question you tried to date earlier didn't close on an afternoon. It stopped tilting, and there's no experience of a thing ceasing to tilt.

Standing on a floor is also what the belief-first view was describing. You reason from a claim rather than about it because there's nowhere to reason to. The alternatives aren't being held down; they just aren't downhill of you. It also explains the kind of hold a belief has on you, the one that view calls *defeasible*. A switch doesn't hold anything defeasibly. A floor with walls around it does exactly that: you stay until something arrives with enough force to put you up a side, and how much force depends on the side.

A floor is the yes-or-no part. Either the ground under you still tilts and you're still rolling, or nothing leads lower and you've stopped. Valleys also differ in how hard they are to get out of, and that's a different fact about the same place: how much evidence it would take to move you.[^c2-resilience] Call it depth for short, though a valley can be hard to leave because its floor sits low or because its walls are steep, and nothing here turns on which. A shallow floor is still a floor. That suggests reading belief and confidence as two questions you can put to the same settled state. Ask whether you've stopped, whether any small move still leads lower, and you're asking about belief. Ask how much it would take to get you out, and you're asking about confidence.[^c2-onlyconfidence]

How hard you'd be to get out is roughly what *confidence* meant before anyone recorded a neuron: to be confident of something is to be hard to talk out of it. It isn't either of the quantities we watched earlier. The climb in LIP stops at the same height every time, and the hump in Pouget's populations is certainty about what you're looking at, not about how hard you'd be to move.[^c2-candidates]

Go back to the metal from earlier. It showed only that one thing can have two independent features; the landscape offers a candidate for the one thing. Nobody worries about how a material's hardness and its toughness are kept in step, or wonders which of them it *really* has. They're two things you can ask about how a lump of stuff answers force. Press on it and see whether it dents; strike it and see whether it cracks. One object, two questions, and nothing to coordinate.[^c2-coordinate]

None of this decides whether there are two states or one state with two features. What the landscape adds is a single process that produces both the weighing and the settling, and that's why one state with two questions is the reading I'm proposing. The biology supports it. It doesn't settle it.

That leaves the cost of computing, the strongest reason anyone gave for closing questions at all.

The landscape doesn't make the computing cheap. It moves it: most of the computing was done in advance. Learning builds what the world kept doing into the shape of the terrain, slowly, over every encounter, and what's left is a surface that falls the right way. A trained system settling into a state may be doing something a probability theorist can describe well from outside, without working out a probability for every possibility each time it's asked. The expense was real and it was paid, during the carving, and it doesn't have to be paid again each time a question comes up.

Once that's paid, the obstacle to holding everything open isn't only the arithmetic. Holding a question open means holding a state the terrain doesn't support, standing on a slope and refusing to roll.

The station example put this in numbers, but nobody needs the numbers. Try spending the first ten minutes of tomorrow with nothing settled. Before you stand, weigh whether the floor will take your weight. Before you drink, ask whether what's in the glass is water. Is that your toothbrush? Is the staircase where it was last night? Each of those is a fair question, and an open mind would give each its due. You'd still be on the edge of the bed at noon, and you'd know it long before then, not as a sum too large to finish but as being unable to move. Nobody can stand on the slope across the board. In a few places, with effort, you can.

## Sparse, local, and made of its own history

There's a question I can ask you that you have never once considered. Is the number of trees in Canada odd or even?

You'll say fifty-fifty, and you're right to. Notice what just happened. You produced that answer. You didn't consult it. A moment ago there was nothing in you about the parity of Canadian trees, and now there's a number, made by the asking.

That's the first place where what a brain does and what the ideal describes come apart. The ideal reasoner has a probability distribution, and a distribution covers every possibility, including the ones you've never considered; one with holes in it isn't a distribution. Read as a description of you, that would mean you had a confidence about Canadian trees before I asked, and about the parity of every other countable thing, and about questions nobody has thought to ask yet. I doubt anyone means it literally. The trouble isn't storage. It's that the thing being described isn't there.[^c2-imprecise]

A landscape has no such requirement. It has shape only where learning shaped it. You have a shape for whether it's about to rain, and for whether Fred can be relied on, and for a few thousand other things the world made you deal with repeatedly. Those aren't coordinates in one enormous probability distribution over everything; they're separate pieces of terrain, carved because they were used. Everything else isn't set to a default. It's absent.

That also takes care of the gap at the beginning, from earlier. Bayes' rule tells you how to get from one confidence to the next. It can't tell you where to start, and every model has to be handed its starting point. A landscape never faces the question, because it never sits blank waiting for a starting value. What you know about rain is the shape rain left. Before any of it, there wasn't a starting confidence. There was the brain's starting wiring, which already has a shape of its own, so it isn't nothing, but it isn't a confidence about rain. The shape came later.

So the first difference is that the landscape is **sparse rather than exhaustive**. It holds what got carved into it, not a value for everything sayable. From the inside, that means the questions you have a settled answer to are the ones something carved a floor for. Everything else you answer, when asked, by producing something on the spot, the way you just did with the trees.

The second follows from the first. Different pieces of terrain were cut at different times by different experience, and nothing ever checks them against each other.

Remember the football match from earlier, and being sixty per cent sure the home team will win and sixty per cent sure the away team will. A distribution can't hold both, because it's one object and its parts have to add up. A landscape holds both without strain. One confidence was carved watching the home side play, the other watching the away side, and nothing ever put the two together. There's no step anywhere in the brain where everything gets added up.

That's still a fault. The bookmaker can still take your money. What changes is how surprising it is. For the ideal reasoner, confidences that hang together are the starting condition, and failing to is a lapse. For a learned landscape there was never a whole to hang together, and making two confidences agree takes work: somebody has to put them side by side. People carry conflicting commitments for years, and usually not out of carelessness. The two just never came up in the same afternoon.

The author from the preface fits here too, though not as a fault. Her confidences can hang together perfectly well: each claim very likely, the whole book quite likely to contain an error somewhere. What the landscape adds is a picture of how her outright commitments are organised. Each claim was settled on its own, checked against its own sources, and sits on its own floor. No floor was ever carved for the book as a whole, because she never had to hold every claim in view at once. On a strict yes-or-no reading that has looked like a contradiction; on this one it's ordinary. It describes how her commitments are arranged, not what she ought to believe.

So the second difference is that it's **local rather than all of a piece**. From the inside: each floor holds on its own, whatever its neighbours would say if anyone introduced them.

The third has the most evidence behind it.

In the mid-1970s Lee Ross and his colleagues at Stanford asked students to sort genuine suicide notes from fakes, and then told them how they'd done. The scores were made up. Some students heard they were excellent at it, others that they were poor. Then the experimenters told them so: the feedback had been assigned at random and had nothing to do with their answers. Afterwards, asked how good they thought they really were, the students who'd been told they were excellent still rated themselves well above the ones told they were poor.[^c2-perseverance]

By the evidence, the two groups were back where they started. Everything that had separated them had been taken back. What still separated them was the route. They had each been somewhere, and coming back out didn't return them to the same place.

The plain Bayesian update can't produce that by itself. Evidence that's been withdrawn should leave you where you were before it came, and the same evidence in a different order should leave you in the same place; on the plain update the order washes out.[^c2-jeffrey] People don't work that way, and the order effect has been measured directly too: give people the same pieces of evidence in a different order and they reach different conclusions.[^c2-order]

A Bayesian has a good reply here. A probabilistic model can produce these effects perfectly well: by treating the world as something that might be changing underneath you, by assuming something hidden that the sequence of evidence is telling you about, by being uncertain how much each piece of evidence should count. These are legitimate and they work, and anyone who says probabilistic models can't represent history is wrong.

So the question isn't whether a probability model can produce this. It's where it comes from. If you start from the ideal, order has to be added. The ideal ignores it, people turn out not to, and so the modeller builds in something extra to cover the difference. If you start from a system that learns by being reshaped, nothing has to be added. The first round of feedback changed the surface that the debriefing then ran on. Learning doesn't write an entry in a ledger. It changes the learner.

So the third difference is that it's **historical rather than indifferent to order**. From the inside, this one is the stickiness. A settled state is harder to leave than it was to reach, and how much harder depends on the way you came in.

Put the three beside the ideal and they're exactly where a learned landscape and a probability distribution part company. Nobody assumed them to get this result. They're one fact about anything shaped by learning, seen from three sides: the landscape has shape only where something shaped it, the shaping happened a piece at a time, and it happened in an order. Anything that gets its shape by being carved this way should have all three. A system whose whole distribution is set down at the start, the way the ideal's is, has none of them unless someone adds them.

Look at what they add up to from the inside. A question you have a floor for. A floor that holds whatever its neighbours say. A floor that's harder to leave than it was to reach. That's the settled half of believing, the half the belief-first camp has been describing all along. The places where the brain parts from the ideal are the places where believing stops feeling like a degree.

## What each of them was seeing

Go back to the three camps with all this in hand, and each of them can be read as describing a real part of what one process produces, from the side it happened to be standing on. Seen this way, three arguments that have been talking past each other for forty years are about the same thing happening.

Start with the threshold, the most familiar idea in the subject: you believe something when you're confident enough of it. Picture it the way it's usually pictured, as a dial running from no confidence to certainty, with a line drawn across it near the top. Above the line you believe; below it you don't. Now think of the monkey watching the dots. The neurons climb, and when the climb reaches a certain height the eyes move. From outside, from the behaviour alone, that's a line on a dial: below some level of evidence the animal keeps watching, and above it the animal commits. The threshold is where the climb stops.

What the dial leaves out is history. The students in the debriefing study would get the same reading on any dial you could build from their evidence, and they weren't in the same state. Where each of them was depended on where they'd been.

There's a familiar machine that behaves this way with no valleys anywhere: a thermostat. Set it to switch the heat on at eighteen degrees and off at twenty. At nineteen it doesn't switch anything, but it isn't always in the same state there. If the room has been cooling from warm, the heat is still off; if it's been warming from cold, the heat is still on. That's the dial with two lines on it instead of one, and a threshold theorist could adopt it tomorrow. Serious ones have already gone part of the way, letting the line move with what's at stake.[^c2-lockean] What the thermostat doesn't have is a reason for its two numbers. Somebody chose them, and another pair would have done as well. Nobody chose the gap between the students. It came from what the first round of feedback did to them, and you couldn't remove it without undoing that.

The credence side has its own way of saying the settled thing. On one version of it, believing something is having a confidence in it that isn't only high but stable: it would stay high whatever you learned next, as long as it was something you hadn't already ruled out.[^c2-leitgeb] That's depth, said in the language of probability. How much it would take to move a state is how deep its valley is, and a confidence that stays high whatever arrives is a state at the bottom of a deep one. The language of degrees turns out to reach the settled half without strain.

The belief-first observations, that you reason from what you believe rather than about it, and that a belief, like an intention, resists being reopened,[^c2-holton] describe standing on a flat floor. You reason from the claim because nowhere nearby is lower. You don't reconsider because reconsidering would mean climbing.

The bus case describes the route. The market share and the witness lead toward the same conclusion by different paths, and leave you differently placed. That's why the higher figure isn't the one you'll believe on.

And the dualist's two attitudes line up with the two questions you can put to a settled state: whether it has stopped, and how much it would take to move it. The dualists were right that confidence and outright belief come apart, in the bus case and in the preface. What the biology offers is one process that produces both.

Unification is cheap. Anyone can stipulate one state with two aspects; it's coherent, it's simpler, and it explains nothing. What gives this proposal some weight is that the process behind it was in a position to fail and didn't. The ramp could have climbed on something other than the weight of the evidence, and it didn't. The category boundary could have sat still regardless of which direction the listener approached from, and it moved. The accumulation could have stopped at the edge of sensory cortex, and it didn't. Freedman's neurons could have tracked the pictures they were shown, and instead they tracked a line an experimenter invented and followed it when he moved it. None of that had to come out that way. None of it pits one state against two, either. What it could have shown, and didn't, is that the weighing and the settling come from different processes.

None of this touches the Bayesian norms, which never belonged to one camp anyway. Probability theory is the right way to reason about uncertainty whether or not it's the form anything is stored in. Planets don't solve differential equations, and the equations are still how you find out where Mars will be. Everything in Bayesian epistemology offered as an account of how confidence *ought* to behave stands where it stood, because none of this is about what anyone ought to do.

So if you started out thinking believing is yes or no, you were right about what you saw. Questions do close, the closed ones get used flat, and the biology shows where the closing comes from. If you thought it comes in degrees, you were right too, and the graded half is as real in the recordings as it was in the evening spent waiting for a friend. The language of probability, which none of the camps owned, fits what the brain does remarkably well. And if you thought there were two things in there, and wouldn't let either explain the other away, there really are two things to say about a belief, and they come out of one process. Whether that makes them two questions about one state, as I've proposed, is still open to argument.

## What the shape can't tell you

The largest debt is one I've been carrying since the first essay and am not going to pay here.

The geometry offers an account of how believing comes about: a settled state with a shape, arrived at by a route, hard to leave in proportion to what's around it. It tells you nothing about what any particular belief is *about*. Your keys being on the counter is a state that could be true or false, that stands in logical relations to other things you hold, and that concerns some keys and some counter out in the world. A valley considered on its own has none of that in it. Where aboutness comes from is a question with a long history and no settled answer. Some locate it in the state's causal links to the world, some in its relations to everything else you hold, some in what the state is for, and I have nothing to add to any of them.[^c2-semantic]

So there are two questions, and this essay works on one. *How does believing come about?* As a settled state with a shape, if the proposal here is right. *What is any particular belief about?* A different question, left open. Leaving it open costs nothing here. An older argument ran the two together: what you believe is a statement, a statement is either true or false, so the state that holds it must be either on or off. The last step doesn't follow. A state can be about something that's true or false without being all-or-nothing itself, and keeping the two questions apart is all this essay needs.[^c2-owed]

Two smaller limits.

Nothing here tells you how you ought to reason.

And what passes between people is already yes-or-no, while what produced it never was. That gap is a later essay's subject.

## What the admiration was actually for

So: you can't trust anyone over thirty.

I think the admiration was half right, and wrong in an interesting place. I used to take the people who keep asking to be running a different kind of mind: more honest, more supple, somehow less prone to the closing that happens to everyone else. They aren't. They're running the same brain on the same terms, and what they're doing is expensive. Every settled question is a valley, and climbing out of one costs something. You give up the floor you were reasoning from, and you keep paying for as long as you stand on the slope instead of rolling back. Nobody can do that everywhere, and a mind that tried would not be admirable. It would be unable to leave the house. What the people I admire do is choose. They pick a few valleys worth climbing out of, and they keep paying for those.

**[D4 — reconstructed turn, tied to choosing which valleys to climb out of. 12 Oct: Ariel is replacing the wording.]**

Which means the moment a question closes was never going to be there to find. There was a slope that got a little steeper each time the world agreed, and no announcement when it got steep enough that you stopped checking.

What would count against the picture gets a proper answer in the fourth essay.[^c2-falsify]

The next one goes somewhere the same shape turns up in much less comfortable company. Moral judgment shows the same route-dependence, the same settling, the same boundary that moves with the direction you came from, and there the gap between describing how people decide and saying anything about how they should is going to take more care, not less.

Their claim is that credences are a species of belief, with the degree fixed by the content believed rather than by a separate graded attitude. Jackson's own position on the wider question is dualist rather than belief-first — see her "A Defense of Belief-Credence Dualism" and the survey in "The Relationship Between Belief and Credence," *Philosophy Compass* 15, no. 6 (2020) — which is worth knowing, because the reduction defended in the 2020 paper and the dualism she argues for elsewhere are answers to different questions.

[^c2-essay1]: The first essay develops this at length and the summary here is deliberately thin; the direction of argument it sets out — philosophy supplies the question, the mechanism is downstream — holds for this essay too.

[^c2-manifold]: The finding that population activity occupies a low-dimensional surface within a much higher-dimensional space of possible firing patterns is now standard in systems neuroscience; see Cunningham and Yu, "Dimensionality Reduction for Large-Scale Neural Recordings," *Nature Neuroscience* 17 (2014), and Churchland et al. on rotational dynamics in motor cortex. The number of dimensions recovered depends on the task and on the analysis, which is part of the point: the surface is shaped by what the network does, not fixed by how many neurons it has.

[^c2-drift]: See Roitman and Shadlen, "Response of Neurons in the Lateral Intraparietal Area during a Combined Visual Discrimination Reaction Time Task," *Journal of Neuroscience* 22, no. 21 (2002); Gold and Shadlen, "The Neural Basis of Decision Making," *Annual Review of Neuroscience* 30 (2007). The pattern, usually modelled as drift diffusion, is among the most replicated findings in decision neuroscience.

[^c2-logLR]: Joshua I. Gold and Michael N. Shadlen, "The Neural Basis of Decision Making," *Annual Review of Neuroscience* 30 (2007): 535–574, where, for conditionally independent pieces of evidence, the decision variable is the logarithm of the likelihood ratio, "the sum of the logLRs associated with each piece of evidence," accumulated to a bound. The later experiment is Tianming Yang and Michael N. Shadlen, "Probabilistic Reasoning by Neurons," *Nature* 447, no. 7148 (2007): 1075–1080: monkeys viewed sequences of four shapes, each assigned a fixed weight of evidence about which choice would be rewarded, and LIP activity tracked the accumulated log likelihood ratio. The sum of the assigned weights is approximately proportional to the log likelihood ratio during the sequence and exactly equal to it after all four shapes. The main text says the activity "behaves very much like" the running total for a reason: what is recorded is firing that tracks the total, not a measured logarithm.

[^c2-pfc]: See Kim and Shadlen, "Neural Correlates of a Decision in the Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex of the Macaque," *Nature Neuroscience* 2, no. 2 (1999); and Freedman, Riesenhuber, Poggio, and Miller, "Categorical Representation of Visual Stimuli in the Primate Prefrontal Cortex," *Science* 291 (2001), including the finding that the neural boundary follows a retrained category boundary rather than the physical stimulus.

[^c2-hysteresis]: The route-dependence described here is a behavioural result, and a robust one: Tuller, Case, Ding, and Kelso, "The Nonlinear Dynamics of Speech Categorization," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Human Perception and Performance* 20, no. 1 (1994). Neural recordings during morphed-stimulus paradigms show category-tuned populations of the kind the behavioural effect would sit on, but do not themselves establish that the neural population carries the route-dependence; see Akrami, Liu, Treves, and Jagadeesh, *Cerebral Cortex* 19, no. 4 (2009), and McKee, Riesenhuber, Miller, and Freedman, *Journal of Neuroscience* 34, no. 48 (2014). Analogous history effects do appear neurally in bistable perception — the Necker cube and its relatives, where an unchanging image flips and what you saw a moment ago biases what you see next.

[^c2-pouget]: See Ma, Beck, Latham, and Pouget, "Bayesian Inference with Probabilistic Population Codes," *Nature Neuroscience* 9 (2006); Pouget, Beck, Ma, and Latham, "Probabilistic Brains," *Nature Neuroscience* 16 (2013).

[^c2-pouget-scope]: A second caveat belongs here too. The step from recorded activity to "the population encodes a distribution" rests on decoding assumptions that are themselves disputed, and the finding is standardly reported as activity *consistent with* probabilistic coding rather than as a direct measurement of it. Nothing in the main line needs the stronger reading.

[^c2-mcclelland]: James L. McClelland, "Integrating Probabilistic Models of Perception and Interactive Neural Networks: A Historical and Tutorial Review," *Frontiers in Psychology* 4 (2013): 503: "connectionist units that use the logistic or softmax activation functions can exactly compute Bayesian posterior probabilities when the bias terms and connection weights affecting such units are set to the logarithms of appropriate probabilistic quantities," and "a simple winner-take-all procedure that could be implemented in real neural circuits can approximate probability matching." That supervised training can approximate posterior probabilities without anyone choosing the weights is the classic result of Michael D. Richard and Richard P. Lippmann, "Neural Network Classifiers Estimate Bayesian a Posteriori Probabilities," *Neural Computation* 3, no. 4 (1991): 461–483: a network with one output per class, trained on one-hot labelled examples to minimise squared error or cross-entropy, can approximate the posterior probability of each class, provided the data are plentiful and representative, the network is large enough and training reaches the minimum. The handwritten digits in the main text illustrate that result; they are not an experiment from the paper.

[^c2-wang]: Siyu Wang, Rossella Falcone, Barry Richmond and Bruno B. Averbeck, "Attractor Dynamics Reflect Decision Confidence in Macaque Prefrontal Cortex," *Nature Neuroscience* 26, no. 11 (2023): 1970–1980. The authors' shared affiliation was the National Institute of Mental Health. Two male rhesus macaques accepted or rejected offers varying in reward size and delay. The landscape was estimated by projecting population activity onto a one-dimensional choice axis defined by a support-vector-machine decoder, estimating the mean temporal derivative along that axis, and spatially integrating the resulting flow field. A fitted dynamical model's retraction coefficient also quantified basin steepness. These are model-based potential estimates, not measurements of physical energy. From the abstract: "the attractor basins around patterns of activity reflecting decisions had steeper landscapes for offers that led to consistent decisions," and "energy landscapes predict decision consistency, which reflects decision confidence." Confidence was not measured directly; it is inferred from choice consistency.

[^c2-resilience]: This is the everyday sense, in which being confident of something and being hard to talk out of it go together. Formal epistemology separates the two: how probable you take something to be, and how well that estimate would hold up under new evidence, which Brian Skyrms called its resiliency ("Resiliency, Propensities, and Causal Necessity," *Journal of Philosophy* 74, no. 11 (1977): 704–713). A coin you've watched land ten thousand times gets an even chance, held very firmly. *Confidence* here means the second, which is also what Leitgeb's stable belief builds in.

[^c2-company]: The idea that probability-like behaviour falls out of network dynamics has a long history in cognitive science; McClelland (2013) is a tutorial review. Matt Jones and Bradley Love, "Bayesian Fundamentalism or Enlightenment?," *Behavioral and Brain Sciences* 34 (2011): 169–188, argue that Bayesian models "must be developed in conjunction with mechanistic considerations to offer substantive explanations of cognition."

[^c2-imprecise]: James Joyce, "A Defense of Imprecise Credences in Inference and Decision Making," *Philosophical Perspectives* 24 (2010), is the canonical philosophical defence. Eriksson and Hájek (2007) are sometimes cited here and are arguing something different: their target is the reduction of degree of belief to betting behaviour, preference, or decision dispositions, and their conclusion is that degree of belief should be treated as a primitive. That bears on this essay from another direction, since it makes a precise numerical credence hard to identify with any single behavioural disposition. The imprecise-credence programme is itself contested from both sides — Mayo-Wilson and Wheeler on its tension with strictly proper scoring rules, White's evidential-symmetry objection and the replies to it. It is a live debate and neither side should be presented as settled.

[^c2-perseverance]: Lee Ross, Mark R. Lepper and Michael Hubbard, "Perseverance in Self-Perception and Social Perception: Biased Attributional Processes in the Debriefing Paradigm," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 32, no. 5 (1975): 880–892. In Experiment 2, Stanford undergraduates judged which of a set of suicide notes were authentic and received predetermined success or failure feedback; after a debriefing explaining that the feedback had been fixed in advance, their estimates of their own ability still followed it. Cathy McFarland, Adeline Cheam and Roger Buehler, "The Perseverance Effect in the Debriefing Paradigm: Replication and Extension," *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 43, no. 2 (2007): 233–240 (published online in 2006), replicated perseverance after the standard outcome debriefing; a revised debriefing discrediting both the test and the feedback eliminated it.

[^c2-jeffrey]: Strict conditionalization is order-invariant: the posterior is a function of the prior and the total evidence, not of the sequence in which it arrived. Jeffrey conditionalization, which updates on uncertain evidence, is not — the result can depend on the order in which the shifts are applied, and this is well known. A Bayesian model can also produce order effects by representing the history explicitly, through a changing latent state or hierarchical structure; the main text's point is only that something has to represent it.

[^c2-order]: Robin M. Hogarth and Hillel J. Einhorn, "Order Effects in Belief Updating: The Belief-Adjustment Model," *Cognitive Psychology* 24 (1992): 1–55. Their review finds order effects across many tasks; whether the earlier or the later evidence dominates depends on the task, on how much evidence there is, and on whether it is evaluated item by item or all at once.

[^c2-lockean]: Richard Foley, *Working Without a Net* (Oxford University Press, 1993), is the usual source for the Lockean thesis. For a stakes-sensitive account of belief, see Brian Weatherson, "Can We Do without Pragmatic Encroachment?," *Philosophical Perspectives* 19 (2005): 417–443.

[^c2-leitgeb]: Hannes Leitgeb, *The Stability of Belief: How Rational Belief Coheres with Probability* (Oxford University Press, 2017). The central principle, which Leitgeb calls the Humean thesis, constrains a perfectly rational agent's beliefs and credences at a time: for a contextually determined threshold r with ½ ≤ r < 1, the agent believes X just in case P(X|Y) > r for every Y with P(Y) > 0 whose negation the agent does not believe. Belief, on this account, is stably high credence. Whether stable belief so defined maximises expected accuracy is disputed; see Minkyung Wang, "Credence and Belief: Epistemic Decision Theory Revisited," *Philosophical Studies* 182 (2025): 1433–1465.

[^c2-holton]: Richard Holton, "Intention as a Model for Belief," in Manuel Vargas and Gideon Yaffe (eds.), *Rational and Social Agency: The Philosophy of Michael Bratman* (Oxford University Press, 2014). Holton extends Bratman's account of intention to belief: beliefs are enduring states that settle matters for planning and coordination in cognitively limited agents, and part of what makes them beliefs is their resistance to reconsideration. He also questions whether credences, as usually characterised, are states we genuinely have.

[^c2-semantic]: The worry about aboutness — that what makes a state a belief is its content rather than its dynamics — belongs to the philosophy of language rather than to neuroscience, and the candidate answers disagree deeply about where content comes from: from a term's place in a structured system of thought (Frege), from its inferential relations to one's other commitments (Brandom), from causal or covariational links to the world (Dretske, Fodor), or from what the state is for (Millikan). Nothing in this essay adjudicates between them, and the argument doesn't require any particular one. Nor does anything here rule out a dualism built on what states are about rather than on how they behave. If what makes a state a belief is something about what it represents rather than how it holds up under pressure, this essay hasn't touched it.

[^c2-owed]: Whether the second question is genuinely a different kind of question or only a harder version of the first is itself disputed, and the dispute has the same shape as the one about consciousness. The structural parallel is the dispute between Daniel Dennett and David Chalmers over consciousness. Chalmers holds that the question why there is something it is like to undergo a physical process differs in kind from the tractable problems of explaining discrimination, integration and report; Dennett holds that the appearance of a residue is an artifact that dissolves as the tractable work gets done. Whether aboutness stands to the geometry as Chalmers's hard problem stands to the easy ones, or only appears to, is what a later essay takes up.

[^c2-falsify]: In outline, since the fourth essay does this properly: if the architecture is attractor-shaped, the framework expects behavioural route-dependence, asymmetry in transitions, and answers clustering at two values rather than spreading evenly in the region between two settled states, across cognitive domains rather than in perception alone. Their absence in well-designed paradigms would count against it. More specifically, a category-transition task in which the boundary did not shift with direction of approach once other variables were controlled, or evidence that confidence updating proceeds with none of the route-dependence described here, would put the architectural claim in trouble.

[^c2-onlyconfidence]: This is worth separating from what the credence-first view usually gets heard as saying. "Believing is only confidence" can mean that one number decides, so that anyone at the same confidence is in the same position. It can mean that one vocabulary suffices, so that anything worth saying about belief can be said in the language of confidence. Or it can mean what the floor and the depth suggest: one state, and two questions. Most of the serious arguments against the credence-first view are aimed at the first meaning, and they land. Two people can be equally confident and still not be in the same position. That shows one number isn't the whole story. It doesn't show that the story needs two states to tell it.

[^c2-candidates]: The word *confidence* has had three candidates by this point. The climb in LIP is evidence being added up while the question is still open: the marble rolling, not yet resting anywhere. Its height can't be how firmly the answer is held, because the climb stops at the same height every time. The hump in Pouget's populations is how sharply a single reading is taken, a dim line against a crisp one; it's certainty about what you're looking at, not about how hard you'd be to move. Depth, how much it would take to move you, is the one that belongs to a settled state. The other two aren't rivals to it. The climb, repeated over many passes in the same direction, is how a valley gets deeper, and the hump shows that a best guess and a degree of certainty can ride in one signal.

[^c2-coordinate]: Coordination is what the dualist's picture has to supply. If belief and confidence are separate states, something has to keep them in step: your confidence that the train leaves at eight falls, and at some point your belief that it leaves at eight has to go too. That's a real task for the dualist, and there are proposals for how to meet it. On the two-questions reading the task doesn't arise.
