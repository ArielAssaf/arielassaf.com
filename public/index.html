The Opacity of the Obvious

Previous in the series: The Attractor Hypothesis. Next in the series: What convinced us. Obviousness Arrives and Erases Its Own History When I first studied economics, its most basic ideas were, for months, actively counterintuitive. I could follow the definitions, but to work with them I had to hold them in place by force. Then the feeling turned over. The ideas didn’t just come to seem correct; they came to seem obvious, and I lost the ability to recover what had ever been hard about them. The obviousness arrived and erased its own history. I can still make the case for it: ask me why this way of modeling an equilibrium is the compelling one and the reasons come readily. But I had those reasons in hand long before they convinced me; I could derive the result while it still felt wrong. So what I’ve started to doubt is whether the reasons I now offer are what convinced me, or something I assemble now to answer the question. The unsettling part isn’t that I can’t produce reasons. It’s that the reasons I produce may be partly built after the fact, and the conviction may never have come from them at all. ...

Previous in the series: The Slopes of Approval. Next in the series: The Opacity of the Obvious. Three Essays, One Shape Three essays in, the same shape kept appearing. The essay on concepts described valleys in a learned landscape: sloped sides, fuzzy edges, and the deepest valleys under the most typical cases, the prototypes. The essay on belief found the same shape under a different name. Confidence comes in degrees and still feels all-or-nothing once it settles; the point where you switch from one view to another moves depending on which side you come from; and how you got into a belief affects how easily you can leave it. The essay on moral sentiment found it again: approval and disapproval as valleys, and moral dilemmas sitting at the saddle points, the mountain passes where two valleys meet and pull equally, so that a small thing decides which way you go. Each time, a theory that wanted clean lines was pressing against ground that wouldn’t hold them.
